Forces explain why things start moving, speed up, slow down, turn or stay still. Isaac Newton summed up how forces affect motion in three laws, which are still used to design cars, rockets and rugby boots. This topic covers those laws and how to use $F = ma$.

## Forces, mass and weight

A **force** is a push or a pull. Forces are measured in **newtons (N)** using a **force meter** (also called a spring balance or newton meter).

- **Contact forces** act when objects touch: **friction**, **air resistance** (drag), water resistance, the **support force** from a surface, tension in a rope, and thrust.
- **Non-contact forces** act at a distance: **gravity**, magnetic force and electrostatic (static electricity) force.

**Mass** and **weight** are different:

| | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The amount of matter in an object | The force of gravity pulling on the object |
| Unit | kilograms (kg) | newtons (N) |
| Changes on the Moon? | No | Yes, it's about one-sixth as much |

$$\text{weight} = \text{mass} \times g \qquad W = mg$$

On Earth, $g = 10$ N/kg (each kilogram is pulled down with 10 N). On the Moon, $g = 1.6$ N/kg. A 50 kg student weighs $50 \times 10 = 500$ N on Earth, but only $50 \times 1.6 = 80$ N on the Moon. Their mass is still 50 kg.

## Free-body diagrams, net force and Newton's first law

A **free-body diagram** shows all the forces on **one** object. Draw the object as a box, then add an arrow for each force: it points in the force's direction, its length shows the size, and it is labelled.

The **net force** is the overall force once all the forces are combined.
- Forces in the **same** direction **add**: 30 N + 20 N to the right = 50 N to the right.
- Forces in **opposite** directions **subtract**: 30 N right and 20 N left gives a net force of 10 N to the right.

If the net force is zero, the forces are **balanced**. If not, they are **unbalanced**.

**Newton's first law:** an object stays still, or keeps moving at a constant velocity (same speed, same direction), unless an **unbalanced force** acts on it.

- Balanced forces: no change in motion. A car cruising at a steady 100 km/h has balanced forces, with thrust equal to drag.
- Unbalanced forces: the object speeds up, slows down or changes direction.

**Inertia** is an object's resistance to changes in its motion. The more mass, the more inertia: a loaded truck is much harder to start or stop than a bike. When a bus brakes suddenly, your body keeps moving forward because of its inertia. Seatbelts give you the force you need to slow down with the bus.

## Newton's second law: F = ma

When the forces are unbalanced, the object accelerates. **Newton's second law** links the net force, mass and acceleration:

$$F = ma \qquad \text{net force (N)} = \text{mass (kg)} \times \text{acceleration (m/s}^2\text{)}$$

Rearranged: $a = \dfrac{F}{m}$ and $m = \dfrac{F}{a}$.

- A bigger net force gives a bigger acceleration.
- A bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration for the same force.

### Example

A 1,200 kg car accelerates at 2 m/s². What net force acts on it?

$F = ma = 1200 \times 2 = 2400$ N

### Example

A car of mass 800 kg has a driving force of 3,000 N and friction plus air resistance of 600 N. What is its acceleration?

1. Net force: $3000 - 600 = 2400$ N
2. $a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{2400}{800} = 3$ m/s²

> **Watch out:** $F$ in $F = ma$ is the **net** force. Always find the net force first when there's more than one force.

## Newton's third law: force pairs

**Newton's third law:** when object A pushes or pulls on object B, object B pushes or pulls on object A with a force that is **equal in size** and **opposite in direction**.

The two forces in a pair:
- act on **different** objects,
- are the **same type** of force,
- are always equal in size.

| Action | Reaction |
|---|---|
| A swimmer pushes the water backwards | The water pushes the swimmer forwards |
| Your foot pushes the ground backwards as you walk | The ground pushes your foot forwards |
| A rocket pushes hot gases down | The gases push the rocket up |
| A paddle pushes water backwards | The water pushes the paddle (and the waka) forwards |
| The Earth pulls you down (your weight) | You pull the Earth up |

> **Watch out:** A book resting on a table has weight (down) and a support force (up). These are equal, but they are **not** a third-law pair: they both act on the book, and they are different types of force. They are balanced forces (first law).

## Friction, air resistance and terminal velocity

**Friction** acts between surfaces and always opposes motion. It can be useful (tyre grip, brakes, shoe soles) or a nuisance (it wears parts out and wastes energy as heat). Lubricants such as oil, smoother surfaces, and wheels or ball bearings all reduce friction.

**Air resistance** (drag) is friction from the air. It gets **bigger as the speed increases**. A **streamlined** shape, like a cyclist crouching low, reduces drag. **Water resistance** works the same way.

**Terminal velocity:** when a skydiver jumps,
1. at first their weight is much bigger than the air resistance, so they speed up;
2. as they speed up, air resistance increases, so the net force gets smaller and they speed up more slowly;
3. eventually air resistance equals weight. The forces are **balanced**, so they fall at a steady speed called **terminal velocity**.

When the parachute opens, air resistance suddenly becomes bigger than the weight. The net force is upwards, so the skydiver **slows down** (they don't go up). As they slow down, air resistance falls until it equals the weight again, giving a new, much lower terminal velocity for a safe landing.

## Exam tips

- Forces are in **newtons (N)**; mass is in **kg**. Weight is a force: $W = mg$ with $g = 10$ N/kg.
- Find the **net force** first: add forces in the same direction, subtract opposite ones.
- Balanced forces mean **no change** in motion. That can be moving at a constant speed, not just staying still.
- $F = ma$: write the formula, substitute, and give units (N, kg, m/s²).
- Third-law pairs act on **two different objects**. Weight and support on the same object are **not** a pair.
- For terminal velocity, explain step by step: forces → net force → what happens to speed.
