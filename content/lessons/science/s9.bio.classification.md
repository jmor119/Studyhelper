How can you tell if something is alive? And how do scientists sort the millions of different living things on Earth? In this lesson you'll learn the seven life processes, how to classify organisms and use a dichotomous key, and why so many of New Zealand's plants and animals are found nowhere else.

## What makes something alive?

All living things are made of **cells**, and they all carry out seven life processes. Remember them with **MRS GREN**:

| | Life process | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| M | Movement | Moving the body or part of it (plants move slowly, e.g. leaves turning towards light) |
| R | Respiration | Releasing energy from food inside cells |
| S | Sensitivity | Detecting changes in the surroundings and responding |
| G | Growth | Getting bigger or more complex |
| R | Reproduction | Making more of the same kind of organism |
| E | Excretion | Getting rid of waste made by cells, such as carbon dioxide |
| N | Nutrition | Taking in or making food |

> **Watch out:** Respiration is **not** breathing. It is a chemical reaction in every living cell, including plant cells. Excretion is not the same as getting rid of faeces either. Faeces are undigested food that was never part of the body's cells.

A car moves, uses fuel and gives off waste gases, but it isn't alive. It isn't made of cells, and it can't grow or reproduce by itself.

## Sorting living things

**Classification** means sorting organisms into groups by their shared features. The biggest groups are **kingdoms**: animals, plants, fungi, bacteria and protists. Each kingdom is split into smaller and smaller groups:

**kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species**

A **species** is a group of organisms that can breed together and have fertile young. Every species has a two-part scientific name, written in italics: the **genus** and then the species. The tuatara is *Sphenodon punctatus*.

Animals are split into **vertebrates** (with a backbone) and **invertebrates** (without one).

| Vertebrate group | Key features | NZ example |
|---|---|---|
| Fish | Scales, fins and gills | Snapper (tāmure) |
| Amphibians | Smooth, moist skin with no scales | Archey's frog |
| Reptiles | Dry, scaly skin | Tuatara |
| Birds | Feathers, a beak, hard-shelled eggs | Kiwi |
| Mammals | Hair or fur; feed young on milk | Long-tailed bat (pekapeka) |

Invertebrates include **arthropods**, which have jointed legs and an exoskeleton. Insects such as wētā have 6 legs and 3 body parts. Spiders have 8 legs and 2 body parts. Invertebrates also include **molluscs** (soft bodies, often with a shell, like pāua) and **segmented worms** (like earthworms).

## Dichotomous keys

A **dichotomous key** identifies an organism using pairs of opposite statements. At each step you choose one statement, and it tells you where to go next.

| Step | Statement | Go to / name |
|---|---|---|
| 1a | Has legs | Go to 2 |
| 1b | Has no legs | Go to 3 |
| 2a | Has 6 legs | Wētā |
| 2b | Has 8 legs | Katipō (spider) |
| 3a | Body made of many rings (segments) | Earthworm |
| 3b | Body not in segments; has a shell | Snail |

### Example

An animal has no legs, and its body is made of many rings. Step 1b sends you to step 3, and 3a gives **earthworm**.

> **Tip:** Good keys use clear features that don't change, such as the number of legs, wings or body parts. Avoid statements like "is it big?" or "is it fast?", because people will answer them differently.

## Adaptations

An **adaptation** is a feature that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its **habitat**. Adaptations develop over many generations, not during one animal's lifetime.

| Type | Meaning | New Zealand example |
|---|---|---|
| Structural | A body feature | Kiwi have nostrils at the tip of their long beak, so they can smell worms in the soil |
| Behavioural | Something the organism does | Kiwi are nocturnal; kākāpō freeze when they sense danger |
| Physiological | A process inside the body | Mountain stone wētā can survive their bodies freezing in winter |

Plants have adaptations too. Sundews grow in boggy ground, where the soil has few nutrients. They have sticky leaves that trap and digest insects, which gives them the nutrients they need.

## New Zealand's special species

- **Endemic** species are found naturally in only one place, e.g. kiwi, tuatara and kauri.
- **Native** species got here without help from people. Some are also found elsewhere: the pūkeko also lives in Australia.
- **Introduced** species were brought by people, e.g. possums, stoats and rats.

New Zealand split from the supercontinent Gondwana about 80 million years ago. Isolated by ocean, its species evolved separately. Before people arrived, the only native land mammals were bats. Birds filled many of the roles that mammals have elsewhere, and many became flightless or nested on the ground.

- **Kiwi**: flightless and nocturnal; the only birds with nostrils at the tip of the beak.
- **Kākāpō**: the world's heaviest parrot; flightless and nocturnal.
- **Tuatara**: the last survivors of an ancient group of reptiles that lived alongside the dinosaurs.
- **Wētā**: large, wingless insects; giant wētā are among the heaviest insects in the world.
- **Kauri**: huge endemic conifers. Tāne Mahuta, in Waipoua Forest, is the largest living kauri.

Because these species evolved without mammal predators, introduced stoats, rats, cats and possums are a serious threat to them.

## Exam tips

- Learn MRS GREN, and don't mix up **respiration** with breathing or **excretion** with getting rid of faeces.
- With a key, write down the path you follow (e.g. 1a → 2a → 3b). It shows your working and helps you spot mistakes.
- When you name an adaptation, say how it helps the organism survive. "Kiwi have nostrils at the tip of their beak" is a fact. Adding "so they can smell worms and grubs in the soil" turns it into an explanation.
- Know the difference between endemic, native and introduced, with an example of each.
- When you explain why New Zealand has many endemic species, use the word **isolated** and mention the long time (about 80 million years).
