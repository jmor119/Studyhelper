Light and sound are both **waves**: they carry energy from one place to another without carrying matter with them. But they behave very differently. Sound needs something to travel through, while light can cross empty space. This topic covers how sound and light travel, how they reflect and bend, and how we see colour.

## Sound waves

All sounds are made by something **vibrating** (moving quickly back and forth): a guitar string, a drum skin, your vocal cords.

The vibrating object pushes on the particles next to it, which push on their neighbours, and so on. The sound passes through the material as a **wave**, while each particle just vibrates back and forth around the same spot. When the wave reaches your ear, it makes your **eardrum** vibrate, and you hear the sound.

The material a wave travels through is called a **medium**. Sound can travel through solids, liquids and gases, but **not through a vacuum** (empty space), because there are no particles to pass on the vibrations. In space, no one can hear you shout.

| Medium | Speed of sound (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Air | 340 m/s |
| Water | 1,500 m/s |
| Steel | 5,000 m/s or more |

Sound travels faster in liquids and solids, because the particles are closer together.

## Pitch and loudness

**Frequency** is the number of vibrations (waves) per second, measured in **hertz (Hz)**. 100 Hz means 100 vibrations every second.
- **Higher frequency → higher pitch** (a squeak or whistle).
- **Lower frequency → lower pitch** (a rumble or bass drum).
- Humans can hear about **20 Hz to 20,000 Hz**. Sounds above that are **ultrasound**, which dogs and bats can hear and doctors use for scans.

**Amplitude** is the size of the vibration: how far the particles move from their resting position.
- **Bigger amplitude → louder** sound (more energy).
- Loudness is measured in **decibels (dB)**. Very loud sounds can damage your hearing.

On an oscilloscope trace (a picture of a sound wave):
- **more waves** squeezed across the screen = higher frequency = **higher pitch**;
- **taller waves** = bigger amplitude = **louder**.

## How light travels and reflects

Light travels in **straight lines**, which we draw as **rays**. It is extremely fast, about **300,000 km per second**, and it can travel through a **vacuum**. That's how sunlight reaches us across space.

Because light is so much faster than sound, you see lightning before you hear the thunder. Sound covers about 1 km every 3 seconds.

**Luminous** objects (the Sun, a lamp) give out their own light. We see everything else because light **reflects** off it into our eyes. Light passes through **transparent** materials (glass), partly through **translucent** ones (frosted glass), and not through **opaque** ones. Opaque objects make **shadows**, because light can't bend around them.

**The law of reflection:** the **angle of incidence = the angle of reflection**.

Both angles are measured from the **normal**, an imaginary line drawn at 90° to the mirror where the ray hits it.

> **Watch out:** If a question gives the angle between the ray and the **mirror**, subtract it from 90° to get the angle from the normal. A ray at 25° to the mirror has an angle of incidence of 65°.

Smooth, shiny surfaces like mirrors give a clear reflection (**regular reflection**). Rough surfaces like paper scatter light in all directions (**diffuse reflection**), so you can't see an image in them.

## Refraction

**Refraction** is the bending of light when it passes from one transparent material into another, for example from air into water or glass. It happens because light **changes speed**: it travels more slowly in water and glass than in air.

- Air → glass or water (slows down): the ray bends **towards** the normal.
- Glass or water → air (speeds up): the ray bends **away from** the normal.
- A ray that hits the boundary straight on, along the normal, doesn't bend. It just changes speed.

**Everyday refraction:**
- A straw in a glass of water looks bent at the surface.
- A swimming pool or river looks **shallower** than it really is. Light from the bottom bends as it leaves the water, and your brain assumes the light travelled in a straight line.
- Lenses in glasses, cameras and your eyes use refraction to focus light.

## Colour

**White light** is a mixture of all the colours. A **prism** splits white light into a **spectrum**, because each colour refracts by a slightly different amount. Violet bends the most and red the least. The order is **red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet** (remember "ROY G BIV"). A **rainbow** forms the same way: raindrops refract and reflect sunlight, splitting it into colours.

**How we see colours:** an object **reflects** its own colour and **absorbs** the others.
- A red apple reflects red light and absorbs the rest.
- A white object reflects all colours. A black object absorbs all colours.
- Under blue light, a red T-shirt looks **black**: there is no red light for it to reflect.

A coloured **filter** lets through (transmits) only its own colour and absorbs the rest. A red filter turns white light red.

## Exam tips

- Sound = **vibrations**, and it needs a **medium**. It can't travel through a vacuum. Light can.
- **Frequency ↔ pitch** and **amplitude ↔ loudness**. Don't mix them up.
- Reflection angles are measured from the **normal**, not the mirror. Angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
- Refraction happens because light **changes speed** in a new material.
- Spectrum order: **ROY G BIV**. Objects reflect their own colour and absorb the rest.
- Echo and thunder questions use speed = distance ÷ time. For an echo, the sound travels **there and back**, so halve the total distance.
