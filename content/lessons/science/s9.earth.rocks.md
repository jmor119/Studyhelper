Aotearoa is one of the most geologically active places on Earth. We have the Southern Alps rising along the Alpine Fault, volcanoes from Ruapehu to Whakaari/White Island, and regular earthquakes. This lesson explains what the Earth is made of, how rocks form and change, and why New Zealand shakes and erupts.

## Inside the Earth

The Earth is made of layers, a bit like a peach.

| Layer | What it is like |
|---|---|
| **Crust** | The thin, solid, rocky outer layer. About 5–10 km thick under the oceans, and about 30–50 km thick under the continents. |
| **Mantle** | About 2900 km of very hot rock. It is mostly solid, but it can flow very slowly, like extremely thick toffee. |
| **Outer core** | Liquid iron and nickel. |
| **Inner core** | Solid iron and nickel, over 5000 °C. It stays solid because of the enormous pressure. |

The crust and the very top of the mantle are broken into huge pieces called **tectonic plates**. Slow **convection currents** in the hot mantle help move the plates, by a few centimetres each year, about as fast as your fingernails grow.

> **Watch out:** The mantle is not a sea of liquid magma. It is mostly solid rock that creeps very slowly. Magma forms only in certain places where rock melts.

## Three types of rock

**Igneous rocks** form when molten rock cools and hardens. Molten rock underground is **magma**; once it reaches the surface it is called **lava**.

- Cools **slowly** underground → **large crystals**, e.g. granite. The golden sand of Abel Tasman National Park comes from weathered granite.
- Cools **quickly** at the surface → **small crystals** or glass, e.g. basalt (Rangitoto and many of Auckland's volcanoes), pumice (full of gas bubbles, light enough to float) and obsidian (volcanic glass). Early Māori made sharp cutting tools from obsidian found at places such as Tūhua (Mayor Island).

**Sedimentary rocks** form from sediment, such as bits of rock, sand, mud and shells. The sediment settles in layers, usually under water. Over a long time the layers are squashed (**compacted**) and glued together (**cemented**). Examples: sandstone, mudstone and limestone. Limestone is made mainly from the shells and skeletons of sea creatures; the Pancake Rocks at Punakaiki and the Waitomo Caves are limestone. **Fossils** are found in sedimentary rocks.

**Metamorphic rocks** form when existing rocks are changed by great **heat and pressure** deep underground, *without melting*. Limestone becomes **marble** (as at Tākaka Hill), mudstone becomes slate, and other rocks become schist (common in Central Otago). Pounamu, a taonga for Māori found on Te Tai Poutini (the West Coast), is also a metamorphic stone.

## The rock cycle

Rocks are slowly but constantly being recycled. The **rock cycle** links the three rock types:

1. **Weathering and erosion** break rocks down and carry the pieces away.
2. The sediment is **deposited** in layers, then **compacted and cemented** → **sedimentary** rock.
3. Buried deep, heat and pressure change rock → **metamorphic** rock.
4. If it gets hot enough, rock **melts** → magma.
5. Magma **cools and hardens** → **igneous** rock.
6. Plate movements push rocks back up to the surface (**uplift**), where weathering starts again.

There's no fixed route around the cycle. Any rock can be weathered, any rock can be buried and changed, and any rock can melt. The cycle is powered by the **Sun** (which drives weather and the water cycle at the surface) and by **heat from inside the Earth** (which melts rock and moves plates).

## Plates, earthquakes and volcanoes in Aotearoa

New Zealand sits on the boundary between two plates: the **Pacific Plate** and the **Australian Plate**. The boundary behaves differently in the two main islands.

**North Island: subduction.** Off the east coast, the Pacific Plate is sliding down (**subducting**) beneath the Australian Plate. Deep in the hot mantle, rock melts and **magma** rises towards the surface. This feeds the volcanoes of the **Taupō Volcanic Zone**, which runs from Ruapehu to Whakaari/White Island, along with the geysers and hot pools around Rotorua. Lake Taupō fills a huge crater (a caldera) left by enormous eruptions; the most recent big one was about 1800 years ago.

**South Island: the Alpine Fault.** Here the plates mainly **slide past each other** sideways along the Alpine Fault, which runs for about 600 km. They are also squeezed together, pushing up the **Southern Alps**. Rocks near Nelson match rocks in Otago that are now about 480 km away: they have been dragged apart along the fault over millions of years. The fault produces a very large earthquake every few hundred years. The last one was in 1717.

**Earthquakes** happen when stress builds up in rocks along a **fault** (a crack where rocks move) until the rocks suddenly slip, sending out shaking waves. Large NZ earthquakes include Hawke's Bay (1931), Christchurch (2011) and Kaikōura (2016). GeoNet monitors our earthquakes and volcanoes.

## Weathering, erosion, soil and fossils

**Weathering** breaks rock into smaller pieces where it is:

- **Physical weathering:** water seeps into cracks, freezes and expands, widening the crack (freeze–thaw). Plant roots also prise cracks apart.
- **Chemical weathering:** rainwater is slightly acidic, so it slowly dissolves limestone. Over a very long time this carved out the Waitomo Caves.

**Erosion** is the **moving** of weathered material by rivers, waves, wind, glaciers and gravity (landslides). When the moving water or ice slows down, it drops the sediment (**deposition**). Gravel carried by braided rivers from the Southern Alps built up the Canterbury Plains.

**Soil** is a mixture of weathered rock particles (sand, silt and clay), **humus** (decayed plant and animal material), water, air and living things such as worms, fungi and bacteria. A few centimetres of soil can take hundreds of years to form.

**Fossils** are the preserved remains or traces of living things from long ago: bones, shells, leaf prints, footprints and burrows. Most form when a dead organism is quickly buried by sediment, and minerals slowly replace its hard parts. Fossils are evidence of past life and past environments. Sea-shell fossils high in the mountains show that those rocks formed under the sea and were later pushed up. In undisturbed layers, the **lowest layers are the oldest**.

> **Watch out:** Weathering *breaks* rock; erosion *moves* it.

## Exam tips

- Crust (thin, solid) → mantle (hot, slowly flowing rock) → outer core (liquid) → inner core (solid).
- Igneous: cooled magma or lava. Crystal size tells you the cooling speed. Sedimentary: compacted layers and fossils. Metamorphic: changed by heat and pressure without melting.
- For rock-cycle questions, name the process at each step: weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation, heat and pressure, melting, cooling.
- NZ: Pacific Plate and Australian Plate. North Island: subduction → Taupō Volcanic Zone. South Island: Alpine Fault → earthquakes and the Southern Alps.
- Weathering breaks rock; erosion moves it. Soil = rock particles + humus + water + air + living things.
