Electricity runs almost everything in your day, from your phone charger to the lights at the rugby field. This topic covers static electricity, what current and voltage are, how to draw circuit diagrams, and the big differences between series and parallel circuits.

## Static electricity

All materials are made of atoms. Atoms contain **positive** charges (protons, in the centre of the atom) and **negative** charges (**electrons**, around the outside). Usually there are equal numbers, so an object is **neutral**.

When you rub two insulators together, **electrons** can move from one onto the other:
- the object that **gains** electrons becomes **negatively** charged;
- the object that **loses** electrons becomes **positively** charged.

Only electrons move. The positive charges stay where they are. This build-up of charge that stays in one place is called **static electricity**.

**The rule:** **like charges repel** (push apart) and **unlike charges attract** (pull together).

Rub a balloon on your hair: electrons move from your hair onto the balloon. The balloon becomes negative and your hair positive, so they attract. Two balloons rubbed the same way are both negative, so they repel.

**Lightning** is static electricity on a huge scale: charge builds up in storm clouds until it jumps to the ground (or another cloud) as a giant spark.

## Current, voltage and resistance

**Current** is the flow of electric charge around a circuit. It is measured in **amps (A)** with an **ammeter**.

**Voltage** is the "push" that drives the current. It measures the energy the battery gives the charges. It is measured in **volts (V)** with a **voltmeter**.

**Resistance** is how much a component opposes (resists) the current. It is measured in **ohms (Ω)**. For the same voltage, more resistance means less current.

A current can only flow if there is a **complete circuit**: an unbroken loop from one side of the battery back to the other. A switch works by opening (breaking) or closing (completing) the loop.

**Conductors** let current flow easily: metals such as copper and aluminium, and graphite (pencil "lead"). **Insulators** don't: plastic, rubber, glass, wood and dry air. Wires are made of copper (a conductor) covered in plastic (an insulator), so the current stays in the wire and can't reach you.

| Meter | Measures | Unit | How to connect it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ammeter | Current | amps (A) | In **series**, in the loop itself |
| Voltmeter | Voltage | volts (V) | In **parallel**, across the component |

## Circuit diagrams

Circuit diagrams use standard symbols and straight lines for wires, so anyone can read them.

| Component | Symbol |
|---|---|
| Cell | A long thin line (the + side) next to a short thick line |
| Battery | Two or more cells joined together |
| Lamp (bulb) | A circle with a cross inside |
| Switch (open) | A gap in the wire with a line hinged up from one side |
| Resistor | A small rectangle |
| Ammeter | A circle with **A** inside |
| Voltmeter | A circle with **V** inside |
| Motor | A circle with **M** inside |

A dot on a diagram shows a **junction**, where wires join and the current can split.

On diagrams, current is shown flowing out of the **positive** (+) terminal, around the circuit and back to the **negative** (−) terminal. (The electrons themselves actually drift the other way.)

## Series circuits

In a **series** circuit, all the components are in **one loop**, one after another.

- There is only **one path**, so the **current is the same** everywhere. Every ammeter reads the same.
- The battery's voltage is **shared** between the components. With a 6 V battery and two lamps, the lamp voltages add up to 6 V, e.g. 3 V + 3 V for identical lamps.
- Adding more lamps adds resistance, so the current drops and **every lamp gets dimmer**.
- If one lamp breaks or is removed, the loop is broken and **everything goes off**. Some old Christmas lights did this.

## Parallel circuits

In a **parallel** circuit, the components are on **separate branches**.

- The current **splits** at a junction, and the branch currents **add up** to the total current from the battery. If the main ammeter reads 0.8 A and one branch has 0.3 A, the other branch has 0.5 A.
- **Each branch gets the full battery voltage**. With a 6 V battery, each lamp has 6 V across it.
- So lamps in parallel stay **as bright** as a single lamp would be on its own, and are brighter than the same lamps in series.
- Each branch works **independently**. If one lamp breaks, the others stay on, and each branch can have its own switch.

That's why **houses are wired in parallel**: every appliance gets the full mains voltage, and you can switch one off without turning everything else off.

## Exam tips

- Static: only **electrons** move. Gaining electrons makes an object negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
- Current: amps (A), ammeter **in series**. Voltage: volts (V), voltmeter **in parallel** (across a component).
- **Series:** current the same everywhere; voltage shared; one break turns everything off.
- **Parallel:** current splits and adds up; each branch gets the full voltage; branches work independently.
- Draw circuit diagrams with a ruler, use the standard symbols, and make sure the loop is complete.
