Lemon juice, vinegar and fizzy drinks are acids. Soap, baking soda and oven cleaner are bases. Acids and bases are chemical opposites: mix them and they cancel each other out. This lesson covers how to measure them with the pH scale, how indicators work, and what happens when acids react.

## Acids and bases around you

**Acids** taste sour (but never taste anything in the lab!). They turn blue litmus red, and they have a pH below 7.

| Acid | Where you find it |
|---|---|
| citric acid | lemons, oranges and limes |
| ethanoic acid | vinegar |
| hydrochloric acid | your stomach (it helps digest food) |
| carbonic acid | fizzy drinks |
| sulfuric acid | car batteries |

A **base** is a substance that can cancel out (neutralise) an acid. Metal oxides, metal hydroxides and metal carbonates are bases. A base that **dissolves in water** is called an **alkali**. Alkalis feel soapy, turn red litmus blue, and have a pH above 7.

| Base | Where you find it |
|---|---|
| sodium hydroxide | oven cleaner and drain cleaner |
| ammonia | some household cleaners |
| sodium hydrogen carbonate (baking soda) | baking |
| magnesium hydroxide, calcium carbonate | antacid tablets |
| calcium carbonate (lime) | spread on farm paddocks |

> **Watch out:** Strong acids and alkalis are **corrosive**: they damage skin and eyes. Always wear safety glasses. Alkalis are especially dangerous to eyes.

## The pH scale and indicators

The **pH scale** measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. At Year 9 it runs from 0 to 14.

- pH **below 7**: acidic. The lower the number, the more acidic.
- pH **7**: **neutral**, like pure water.
- pH **above 7**: alkaline. The higher the number, the more alkaline.

| About pH | 1–2 | 3 | 5 | 7 | 8 | 10 | 13–14 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example | stomach acid | vinegar | black coffee | pure water | baking soda solution | soapy water | oven cleaner |

An **indicator** is a substance that changes colour depending on pH.

- **Litmus** paper: blue litmus turns **red** in acids. Red litmus turns **blue** in alkalis. In a neutral solution neither colour changes. Litmus tells you acid or alkali, but not how strong.
- **Universal indicator** is a mixture of indicators with a whole range of colours. Matching the colour to a chart gives the pH:

| Red | Orange | Yellow | Green | Blue | Purple |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| strongly acidic | acidic | weakly acidic | **neutral** | weakly alkaline | strongly alkaline |

> **Tip:** You can make your own indicator from red cabbage. Its juice turns pink-red in acids and green in alkalis.

## Neutralisation

When an acid reacts with a base, they cancel each other out. This is **neutralisation**:

$$\text{acid} + \text{base} \rightarrow \text{salt} + \text{water}$$

In chemistry, a **salt** is the compound made when an acid reacts with a base. Table salt (sodium chloride) is just one example.

**Naming the salt:**

- The **first** part of the name is the metal from the base.
- The **second** part comes from the acid: hydrochloric acid → **chloride**; sulfuric acid → **sulfate**; nitric acid → **nitrate**.

### Example

hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide → sodium chloride + water

sulfuric acid + magnesium oxide → magnesium sulfate + water

**Neutralisation in everyday life:**

- **Indigestion** is often caused by too much stomach acid. Antacid tablets contain a base, such as magnesium hydroxide, that neutralises some of the acid.
- **Farmers** spread lime (calcium carbonate) on soil that is too acidic, which raises the pH so grass and crops grow well.
- Neutralisation is **exothermic**: the mixture warms up.

If you add an alkali to an acid little by little, with universal indicator in the flask, the colour changes from red through orange and yellow to green (neutral), then blue and purple if you add too much.

## Acids with metals and carbonates

**Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen**

magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen

You see fizzing, the metal gets smaller, and the test tube warms up. The gas gives a **squeaky pop** with a lit splint. Fairly reactive metals such as magnesium and zinc react with dilute acids. Copper, silver and gold don't.

**Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide**

calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid → calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

You see fizzing. The gas turns **limewater milky**. This is why vinegar fizzes with baking soda, and why acid rain slowly eats away limestone and marble buildings and statues.

| Reaction | Products | Gas test |
|---|---|---|
| acid + base | salt + water | (no gas) |
| acid + metal | salt + hydrogen | squeaky pop |
| acid + carbonate | salt + water + carbon dioxide | limewater turns milky |

## Exam tips

- Acid: pH below 7, turns blue litmus red. Alkali: pH above 7, turns red litmus blue. Neutral: pH 7.
- Universal indicator: red (strong acid) → green (neutral) → purple (strong alkali).
- Acid + base → salt + water. Name the salt with the metal first, then chloride, sulfate or nitrate.
- Acid + metal → salt + hydrogen (squeaky pop).
- Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide (limewater turns milky).
- Every alkali is a base, but not every base is an alkali. Only bases that dissolve in water are alkalis.
