Why does a huge steel ship float while a small steel bolt sinks? Why do your ears pop driving up a mountain? Both come down to two ideas: **density** and **pressure** in fluids. This topic explains floating and sinking, and how pressure changes in water and air.

## Density

**Density** tells you how much mass is packed into a certain volume. A brick and a block of polystyrene can be the same size, but the brick has much more mass, so it is denser.

$$\text{density} = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{volume}} \qquad \rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

($\rho$ is the Greek letter "rho".) With mass in grams and volume in cm³, density is in **g/cm³**. Scientists also use kg/m³; 1 g/cm³ = 1,000 kg/m³.

| Substance | Density (g/cm³) |
|---|---|
| Cork | about 0.24 |
| Ice | 0.92 |
| Water | 1.00 |
| Sea water | about 1.03 |
| Aluminium | 2.7 |
| Iron | 7.9 |
| Lead | 11.3 |

### Example

A stone has a mass of 75 g and a volume of 30 cm³. $\rho = \dfrac{75}{30} = 2.5$ g/cm³.

Rearranged: $m = \rho \times V$ and $V = \dfrac{m}{\rho}$.

## Measuring density

To find density you need the **mass** (from an electronic balance) and the **volume**.

- **Regular shape** (like a rectangular block): measure it with a ruler. Volume = length × width × height.
- **Irregular shape** (like a stone): use **displacement**. Half-fill a measuring cylinder with water and read the level. Lower the object in and read the new level. The rise is the object's volume.
- **Liquid:** measure a volume in a measuring cylinder and find its mass on the balance (subtract the mass of the empty cylinder).

Remember: **1 mL = 1 cm³**.

### Example

The water level rises from 50 mL to 62 mL when a stone with a mass of 30 g is added.

Volume = 62 − 50 = 12 cm³, so density = $\dfrac{30}{12} = 2.5$ g/cm³.

## Floating and sinking

A **fluid** is anything that can flow: liquids and gases. A fluid pushes up on anything in it. This upward force is called **upthrust** (or **buoyancy**).

- If the upthrust can balance the object's **weight**, the object **floats**. When an object floats, **upthrust = weight**.
- If the weight is bigger than the biggest upthrust the fluid can give, the object **sinks**.

Density gives a quick test:
- less dense than the fluid → **floats**
- denser than the fluid → **sinks**

So ice (0.92 g/cm³) floats on water (1.00 g/cm³), and so does oil.

**Steel ships float** because a ship isn't solid steel. Its hull holds a lot of air, so its **average** density is less than water's. The hull pushes aside (displaces) a huge volume of water, giving a big upthrust.

**Sea water** is denser than fresh water, so it gives more upthrust. Ships sit a little higher in the sea, and you float more easily at the beach than in a lake.

Gases work too: a hot-air balloon rises because hot air is less dense than the cooler air around it.

## Pressure in liquids

**Pressure** describes how concentrated a force is: how much force pushes on each bit of area. The same force on a smaller area gives a bigger pressure. That's why a sharp knife cuts easily and snowshoes stop you sinking into snow.

In a liquid, the pressure comes from the **weight of the liquid above**. So:

- **Pressure increases with depth.** The deeper you go, the more water is pushing down from above.
- Pressure in a fluid pushes in **all directions**, not just down.

**Evidence and examples:**
- Water squirts out **furthest** from the lowest hole in a tall bottle or tank.
- Dams, like the hydro dams on the Waitaki and Clutha rivers, are built **thicker at the bottom**, where the water pressure is greatest.
- Your ears hurt if you dive to the bottom of a deep pool.
- Submarines need very strong hulls to survive deep in the ocean.

## Air pressure

We live at the bottom of an "ocean" of air called the atmosphere. The weight of the air above us causes **atmospheric pressure**, which is about 100 kPa at sea level.

**Air pressure decreases with altitude** (height above sea level), because the higher you go, the less air there is above you. The air also gets thinner, so each breath has less oxygen. That's why climbers on very high mountains often carry oxygen.

**Everyday examples:**
- Your **ears pop** when you drive up into the hills or fly in a plane. The air pressure outside drops, and the air trapped inside your ear adjusts.
- A sealed bag of chips **puffs up** at the top of a mountain, because the outside air pressure is lower than the pressure inside the bag.
- An empty plastic bottle sealed at the top of a mountain gets **crushed** on the way down, because the outside pressure increases.

## Exam tips

- $\rho = \dfrac{m}{V}$, in g/cm³. Find the volume first if you're given a block's measurements or water levels. 1 mL = 1 cm³.
- Floating: **less dense than the fluid**, and **upthrust = weight**. Sinking: denser than the fluid.
- Don't say "heavy things sink". A ship is very heavy but floats, because of its **average density** and the huge volume of water it displaces.
- Liquid pressure **increases with depth**; air pressure **decreases with altitude**. Both are caused by the **weight of the fluid above**.
- Use an example as evidence: dams thicker at the bottom, ears popping, chip packets puffing up.
