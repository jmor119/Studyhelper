Everything around you is made of tiny **particles**, far too small to see: the air you breathe, the water in your drink bottle, even a rugby ball. The **particle model** explains why solids, liquids and gases behave so differently, and what is really happening when ice melts or a puddle dries up.

## The particle model

The particle model has four big ideas:

- All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms or molecules).
- The particles are **always moving**. The more energy they have, the faster they move.
- There are **forces of attraction** between particles that pull them together.
- There is empty space between particles.

The three states of matter are different arrangements of the *same* particles.

| | **Solid** | **Liquid** | **Gas** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Packed closely in a regular pattern | Close together but jumbled | Far apart, random |
| Movement | Vibrate in fixed positions | Slide past each other | Move fast in all directions |
| Attraction | Strong | Weaker | Very weak |
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of its container | Fills its whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Spreads out to fill the container |
| Squashable? | No | Hardly | Yes, easily |

> **Watch out:** Particles in a solid *are* moving. They vibrate on the spot, but they can't move out of their positions.

## Melting, freezing and sublimation

When you heat a substance, its particles gain energy and move faster. When you cool it, they lose energy and slow down.

- **Melting** (solid → liquid): the particles vibrate so much that they break out of their fixed positions and start sliding past each other. Ice melts at 0 °C.
- **Freezing** (liquid → solid): the particles lose energy, and the forces of attraction pull them back into fixed positions. Water freezes at 0 °C, the same temperature it melts at.
- **Sublimation** (solid → gas): some solids turn straight into a gas without melting first. Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) does this at about −78 °C. The reverse, gas straight to solid, is called deposition. Frost forming on a cold morning is an example.

> **Watch out:** The particles themselves do not melt, expand or get bigger. Only their movement and the spaces between them change.

While a substance is melting or boiling, its temperature stays the same, even though heat is still going in. The energy is being used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles, not to make them move faster.

## Evaporation, boiling and condensation

Liquid → gas can happen in two ways.

- **Evaporation** happens only at the *surface* of a liquid, at any temperature. A few particles near the surface have enough energy to escape into the air. A puddle dries up on a sunny day even though it never gets close to 100 °C.
- **Boiling** happens *throughout* the liquid, at one temperature called the **boiling point**. Bubbles of gas form inside the liquid. Water boils at 100 °C at sea level.

Washing on the line dries faster on a warm, windy day with the clothes spread out. Warmth gives the particles more energy, wind carries the water vapour away, and spreading the clothes out gives a bigger surface.

Evaporation also cools things down. The particles with the most energy escape first, so the particles left behind have less energy on average. That is why sweating cools you.

**Condensation** (gas → liquid) is the reverse. When water vapour touches something cold, such as a bathroom mirror, its particles lose energy, slow down and are pulled together into droplets.

## Diffusion and temperature

Put a drop of food colouring into a glass of still water and the colour slowly spreads until the whole glass is evenly coloured. No one stirred it, so what moved the colour?

The particles of colouring and water are always moving randomly and bumping into each other. Over time, the colouring particles spread from where there are lots of them to where there are fewer. This is **diffusion**: the spreading of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration, caused by their random movement.

You see the same thing when a smell, such as the food from a hāngī or someone's perfume, spreads through the air.

- Diffusion is **fastest in gases**, because gas particles move quickly and have lots of space.
- It is slower in liquids, and it doesn't happen noticeably in solids, where particles stay in fixed positions.
- Diffusion is **faster at higher temperatures**, because the particles have more energy and move faster.

> **Tip:** "Higher temperature → particles move faster" explains many things in this topic: faster diffusion, faster evaporation, and expansion when things are heated.

## Physical properties

A **physical property** is something you can observe or measure without turning the substance into a new substance. Examples:

- state at room temperature (solid, liquid or gas)
- melting point and boiling point
- density, hardness and colour
- whether it conducts heat or electricity
- whether it is magnetic or dissolves in water

Scientists group materials by their physical properties. For example, copper, aluminium and steel all conduct electricity, while plastic, wood and rubber don't. Whether something burns (flammability) is a *chemical* property, because burning makes new substances.

### Example: predicting a state

Substance X melts at −20 °C and boils at 60 °C. What state is it at room temperature (20 °C)?

- Below −20 °C it is a solid.
- Between −20 °C and 60 °C it is a liquid.
- Above 60 °C it is a gas.

20 °C is between its melting and boiling points, so X is a **liquid**.

## Exam tips

- Always explain changes using **particles**: their arrangement, their movement (energy) and the forces between them.
- Learn the changes of state and their directions: melting, freezing, evaporation and boiling, condensation, sublimation and deposition.
- Evaporation happens at the surface, at any temperature. Boiling happens throughout the liquid, at the boiling point.
- Heating makes particles move faster and spread further apart. It does **not** make particles bigger.
- Mass stays the same during a change of state. No particles are created or destroyed.
- Diffusion: from high to low concentration, fastest in gases and faster when it is hotter.
