Everything in the universe is built from about a hundred kinds of **atom**. The periodic table organises them so well that scientists used it to predict elements nobody had found yet. Once you can read it, it tells you which elements are metals and which elements behave alike.

## Inside an atom

An **atom** is the smallest particle of an element. Atoms are far too small to see, but they are made of even smaller particles:

| Particle | Charge | Where it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Proton** | positive (+) | in the nucleus |
| **Neutron** | none (neutral) | in the nucleus |
| **Electron** | negative (−) | moving around the nucleus |

The **nucleus** is the tiny, dense centre of the atom. It holds nearly all of the atom's mass. Electrons are about 2000 times lighter than protons, and most of an atom is empty space.

The **atomic number** is the number of protons in an atom. Every element has its own atomic number. For example, every carbon atom has 6 protons, and any atom with 6 protons is carbon.

An atom has the same number of electrons as protons, so the charges cancel out and the atom is **neutral** overall. A carbon atom has 6 protons (+) and 6 electrons (−).

> **NZ link:** Ernest Rutherford, born near Nelson in 1871, showed in 1911 that atoms have a tiny, positively charged nucleus. He's on our \$100 note.

## Elements and their symbols

An **element** is a substance made of only one type of atom. It can't be broken down into anything simpler by a chemical reaction. Gold, oxygen and carbon are elements.

Every element has a **symbol** of one or two letters:

- The first letter is always a **capital**. A second letter is always **lower case**.
- Co is the element cobalt, but CO (two capitals) is carbon monoxide, a compound of carbon and oxygen.

Most symbols come from the English name: H (hydrogen), C (carbon), O (oxygen), Mg (magnesium), Cl (chlorine). Some come from Latin names and must be learnt:

| Element | Symbol | Latin name |
|---|---|---|
| sodium | Na | natrium |
| potassium | K | kalium |
| iron | Fe | ferrum |
| copper | Cu | cuprum |
| silver | Ag | argentum |
| tin | Sn | stannum |
| gold | Au | aurum |
| mercury | Hg | hydrargyrum |
| lead | Pb | plumbum |

> **Tip:** Learn the first 20 elements in order: H, He, Li, Be, B, C, N, O, F, Ne, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar, K, Ca. Their atomic numbers then come for free.

## The periodic table

The modern **periodic table** lists all 118 known elements in order of **atomic number**.

- A **group** is a vertical column. There are 18 groups. Elements in the same group have similar chemical properties.
- A **period** is a horizontal row. There are 7 periods.

### A bit of history

In 1869 the Russian chemist **Dmitri Mendeleev** arranged the elements known at the time in order of their atomic mass, and put elements with similar properties in the same column. His clever move was to **leave gaps** where no known element fitted. He predicted that new elements would be found to fill them, and even predicted their properties.

When gallium (1875) and germanium (1886) were discovered, their properties matched his predictions closely. That was strong evidence that his way of organising the elements was right.

Later, scientists found that ordering elements by **atomic number** (the number of protons) works even better. That is how the modern table is arranged.

> **Tip:** This is how science works. A good idea makes predictions, and when those predictions are tested and turn out true, scientists trust the idea more.

## Metals and non-metals

A zig-zag "staircase" line near the right-hand side of the periodic table separates the metals from the non-metals.

- **Metals** are on the left and in the centre. Most elements are metals.
- **Non-metals** are on the right. **Hydrogen** is the odd one out: it sits at the top left, but it is a non-metal gas.
- A few elements next to the staircase, such as silicon, have some properties of each and are called metalloids.

| Metals | Non-metals |
|---|---|
| Shiny | Dull (when solid) |
| Good conductors of heat and electricity | Poor conductors (insulators) |
| Malleable (can be hammered into shape) and ductile (can be drawn into wires) | Brittle (shatter) when solid |
| Mostly solids with high melting points | Many are gases at room temperature |

There are exceptions. Mercury is a metal that is liquid at room temperature. Graphite, a form of the non-metal carbon, conducts electricity. Bromine is a non-metal that is liquid at room temperature.

## Four important groups

| Group | Name | Examples | Key properties |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Alkali metals** | lithium, sodium, potassium | Soft (can be cut with a knife). React quickly with water, giving off hydrogen gas and making an alkaline solution. Stored under oil. **More reactive further down the group.** |
| 2 | **Alkaline earth metals** | magnesium, calcium | Reactive, but less than Group 1. Harder and denser than Group 1. Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame. **More reactive further down the group.** |
| 17 | **Halogens** | fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine | Very reactive non-metals. React with metals to make salts, e.g. sodium chloride. Colours get darker down the group. **Less reactive further down the group.** |
| 18 | **Noble gases** | helium, neon, argon | Colourless gases that are very unreactive. Uses: helium in balloons, neon in glowing signs, argon inside light bulbs. |

For example, sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen. Potassium, further down Group 1, reacts even more violently, and its hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame.

The halogens change from a pale yellow gas (fluorine) to a yellow-green gas (chlorine), a red-brown liquid (bromine) and a grey-black solid (iodine). Chlorine is used to kill germs in swimming pools and drinking water.

> **Watch out:** The reactivity trends go in opposite directions. Group 1 and 2 metals get **more** reactive down the group, but the halogens get **less** reactive.

## Exam tips

- Proton: +, in the nucleus. Neutron: no charge, in the nucleus. Electron: −, moving around the nucleus.
- Atomic number = number of protons = number of electrons (in a neutral atom).
- Write symbols with a capital first letter and a lower-case second letter. Learn the Latin ones: Na, K, Fe, Cu, Ag, Sn, Au, Hg, Pb.
- Groups are columns (similar properties); periods are rows.
- Metals are on the left and in the centre; non-metals are on the right (plus hydrogen).
- Mendeleev (1869) left gaps and predicted new elements; the modern table is ordered by atomic number.
- Know one property and one use for Groups 1, 2, 17 and 18, and which way reactivity changes down each group.
