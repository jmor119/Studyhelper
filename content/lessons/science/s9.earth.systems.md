Earth is one big connected system. Water, carbon and energy are always moving between the rocks, the oceans, the air and living things. Understanding these cycles explains our weather and climate, why rain is fresh, and why burning fossil fuels is warming the planet.

## Earth's four spheres

Scientists split the Earth system into four "spheres":

| Sphere | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Geosphere** | rocks, soil and minerals, and the inside of the Earth (crust, mantle and core) |
| **Hydrosphere** | all of Earth's water: oceans, rivers, lakes, groundwater and ice |
| **Atmosphere** | the layer of gases around Earth: about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, about 1% argon, and small amounts of carbon dioxide and water vapour |
| **Biosphere** | all living things, and the places where they live |

The spheres are always interacting. For example:

- a volcano (geosphere) puts gases and ash into the air (atmosphere)
- trees (biosphere) take in carbon dioxide from the air (atmosphere)
- rain and rivers (hydrosphere) wear away rock (geosphere).

Most of the energy that drives these cycles comes from the **Sun**. Heat from inside the Earth drives volcanoes and moves tectonic plates.

## The water cycle

Water moves around the Earth in a never-ending loop, powered by the Sun.

1. **Evaporation**: the Sun heats oceans, lakes and rivers, and water evaporates into water vapour. Plants also release water vapour from their leaves (**transpiration**).
2. **Condensation**: warm, moist air rises and cools, and the water vapour condenses into tiny droplets, forming clouds.
3. **Precipitation**: the droplets join together and fall as rain, hail or snow.
4. **Collection**: water runs off the land into rivers and back to the sea, or soaks into the ground to become groundwater.

The Sun provides the energy for evaporation. **Gravity** brings precipitation down and makes rivers flow downhill.

About 97% of Earth's water is salty sea water, and most fresh water is frozen in ice or stored underground. When sea water evaporates, the salt is **left behind**, so rain is fresh water. The water cycle is nature's way of making clean water. Soil, wetlands and plants along riverbanks also help filter water as it flows back to the sea.

## The carbon cycle

Carbon moves between **stores** (also called reservoirs):

- the atmosphere, as carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- living things, in carbohydrates, proteins and fats
- the oceans, as dissolved CO₂
- rocks, as limestone and fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas).

| Processes that **remove** CO₂ from the air | Processes that **add** CO₂ to the air |
|---|---|
| Photosynthesis by plants and algae | Respiration by all living things |
| CO₂ dissolving in the oceans | Decomposition by bacteria and fungi |
| | Combustion (burning wood and fossil fuels) |
| | Volcanic eruptions |

A store that takes in more carbon than it gives out, such as a growing forest, is called a **carbon sink**.

Fossil fuels formed from the remains of ancient living things over millions of years. Burning them releases that stored carbon in just a couple of centuries, faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can take it back up. Cutting down forests also adds CO₂ and removes the trees that would absorb it.

Carbon dioxide and methane are **greenhouse gases**. They absorb heat (infrared radiation) given off by Earth's surface and send some of it back down, keeping the planet warm. This natural **greenhouse effect** is essential: without it, Earth's average temperature would be well below freezing. But extra greenhouse gases from human activity trap more heat, causing **global warming** and **climate change**. In Aotearoa, about half of our greenhouse gas emissions come from farming, mostly methane from cattle and sheep.

> **Watch out:** The greenhouse effect is not the same thing as the hole in the ozone layer. They are different problems.

## Latitude and climate

**Weather** is the conditions on a particular day. **Climate** is the average pattern of weather in a place over a long time (usually 30 years or more).

**Latitude** is how far north or south of the equator a place is, measured in degrees from 0° (the equator) to 90° (the poles). New Zealand lies between about 34° S and 47° S.

**Why are the tropics hotter than the poles?** Earth is a sphere, so sunlight hits different places at different angles.

- Near the **equator**, the Sun is high in the sky and its rays strike the ground almost straight on. The energy is concentrated on a **small area**, so the ground heats up a lot.
- Near the **poles**, the rays arrive at a **low angle**. The same amount of energy is spread over a **much larger area**, and it passes through more atmosphere on the way, so each square metre gets less heat.
- Snow and ice at the poles also reflect most of the sunlight back into space.

This is why Kaitaia (about 35° S) is generally warmer than Invercargill (about 46° S).

> **Tip:** Shine a torch straight down at a desk, then tilt it. The tilted beam makes a bigger, dimmer patch. The light is spread out, just like sunlight near the poles.

## Convection and ocean currents

Warm air and warm water are **less dense**, so they rise. Cool air and water are denser, so they sink. This sets up a circulating flow called a **convection current**.

**Sea breezes:** on a sunny day the land heats up faster than the sea. Warm air rises over the land, and cooler air from over the sea moves in to take its place. That's the cool breeze you feel at the beach in the afternoon.

**Ocean currents** move huge amounts of heat around the planet:

- **Surface currents** are driven mainly by winds. They carry warm water from the tropics towards the poles, warming the coasts they pass. For example, the warm East Auckland Current brings subtropical water to the north-east coast of the North Island.
- **Deep currents** are driven by differences in **density**. Near the poles, sea water becomes very cold, and salty when sea ice forms, so it becomes dense and sinks. It flows slowly along the ocean floor towards the equator and eventually rises again, completing a giant loop.

Water also heats up and cools down much more slowly than land. So places by the sea have **milder climates**, with cooler summers and warmer winters, than places far inland. Coastal Dunedin has a much smaller temperature range than Alexandra in Central Otago, which has hot summers and frosty winters.

## Exam tips

- Learn the four spheres and give an example of two spheres interacting.
- Water cycle: evaporation (and transpiration) → condensation → precipitation → collection. The Sun powers it, and evaporation leaves salt behind.
- Carbon cycle: photosynthesis removes CO₂; respiration, decomposition, combustion and volcanoes add it.
- Explain the tropics being hotter using the **angle** of sunlight and the **area** the energy is spread over, not the distance from the Sun.
- Convection: warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks. Ocean currents carry heat from the equator towards the poles, and the sea gives coasts milder climates.
