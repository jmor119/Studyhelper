Scientists find things out by testing ideas carefully and looking at the evidence. In Year 9 you plan your own investigations: you ask a question, run a fair test, record results, draw a graph and write a conclusion. These skills come up in almost every science test, whatever the topic.

## Questions and hypotheses

An investigation starts with a **testable question**: one you can answer by measuring something. A good testable question links two variables:

> *How does the **height of a ramp** affect **how far a toy car rolls**?*

"Which toy car is the coolest?" is not testable, because "coolest" is an opinion and can't be measured.

A **hypothesis** is a prediction you can test, with a reason. A useful pattern is:

> *If the ramp is higher, then the car will roll further, because it will be going faster at the bottom.*

Your hypothesis doesn't have to be right. If the results show it was wrong, you have still learned something.

## Variables and fair tests

A **variable** is anything that can change in an experiment.

| Variable | What it is | Parachute example |
|---|---|---|
| **Independent variable** | The one you change on purpose | Area of the parachute canopy |
| **Dependent variable** | The one you measure | Time taken to fall |
| **Controlled variables** | Everything you keep the same | Drop height, mass hanging underneath, string length, canopy material |

A **fair test** changes **only the independent variable** and keeps everything else controlled. Then you know any change in the dependent variable was caused by the independent variable.

> **Tip:** "I **change** the independent variable; the dependent variable **depends** on it."

> **Watch out:** If you change two things at once, for example bigger parachutes that are also made of a different material, you can't tell which one caused the difference.

## Recording results: tables and graphs

**Results tables:**
- Put the independent variable in the first column.
- Put **units in the headings**, not after every number, e.g. "Time (s)".
- Repeat each test (usually three times) and calculate the **mean** (average): add the results and divide by how many there are.

An **outlier** (anomalous result) is a result that doesn't fit the pattern, often caused by a mistake. Check it, or repeat it, and leave it out of the mean.

**Graphs:**
- Put the independent variable on the **x-axis** (across) and the dependent variable on the **y-axis** (up).
- Use a **line graph** when both variables are numbers (temperature, time, height).
- Use a **bar graph** when the independent variable is in categories (type of material, brand of paper towel).
- Give the graph a title, label both axes with units, use even scales, and draw a smooth **line of best fit** rather than joining the dots.

## Conclusions and evaluation

A **conclusion** answers your question using your evidence. A strong conclusion:
1. describes the **pattern** (trend): "As the ramp height increased, the distance the car rolled increased."
2. quotes **data**: "At 10 cm the car rolled 0.8 m, but at 50 cm it rolled 3.1 m."
3. says whether the results **support your hypothesis**, and explains why if you can.

An **evaluation** looks at how good your method was.
- **Reliability:** if you repeat the test and get similar results, your results are reliable. Doing repeats and taking a mean makes results more reliable.
- **Sources of error:** reaction time when starting and stopping a stopwatch, reading scales at an angle, or variables you didn't control (like a draught from a window).
- **Improvements:** do more repeats, control more variables, use more precise equipment (a light gate or a video instead of a stopwatch), or make the thing you're timing take longer, so your reaction time matters less.

## How science works

Science is a way of building knowledge from **evidence**.
- Scientists **share** their methods and results (for example in journals and at conferences), so other scientists can check them and try to repeat the experiments.
- Ideas are **tested** again and again. An idea becomes widely accepted when lots of evidence supports it.
- Science ideas **can change** when new evidence turns up. In 1912 Alfred Wegener suggested the continents slowly move. Most scientists rejected the idea because he couldn't explain *how*. In the 1950s and 1960s, maps and measurements of the sea floor gave the missing evidence, and the idea grew into the theory of **plate tectonics**, which explains New Zealand's earthquakes and volcanoes.
- New Zealand scientists are part of this. Ernest Rutherford, who was born near Nelson, used experiments to show that atoms have a tiny nucleus, which changed the model of the atom.

People have built knowledge by careful observation for a long time. For example, Māori observed patterns in the Moon, stars, plants and animals over many generations. The **maramataka** (the Māori lunar calendar) uses these observations to guide activities such as planting and fishing.

## Exam tips

- **Independent** = what you change. **Dependent** = what you measure. **Controlled** = what you keep the same. Learn one full example, such as the parachute.
- A hypothesis is a **prediction with a reason**: "If… then… because…".
- In tables and graphs, units go in the **headings and axis labels**. The independent variable goes on the **x-axis**.
- Line graph for numbers; bar graph for categories.
- Conclusion = **trend + data + hypothesis**.
- "More reliable" almost always means **repeat and average**. Always say *why* an improvement helps.
