No living thing survives on its own. Every organism depends on others for food, shelter or pollination, and on non-living things such as water, light and soil. In this lesson you'll learn how ecosystems are organised, how energy and nutrients move through them, and how people, including through mātauranga Māori, observe and protect Aotearoa's ecosystems.

## Ecosystems and their parts

Ecologists study life at different levels:

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | One living thing | One tūī |
| **Population** | All the organisms of one species in an area | All the tūī in a forest |
| **Community** | All the populations of different species in an area | The tūī, kererū, wētā, trees, fungi… |
| **Ecosystem** | A community plus its non-living environment | The forest, with its soil, streams and climate |

Organisms play different roles:

- **Producers** make their own food by photosynthesis, e.g. trees, ferns and seaweed.
- **Consumers** eat other organisms. **Herbivores** eat plants, **carnivores** eat animals, and **omnivores** eat both.
- **Decomposers**, mainly bacteria and fungi, break down dead organisms and waste. This returns nutrients to the soil.

## Food chains and food webs

A **food chain** shows what eats what. The arrows show the direction **energy flows**, from the food to the eater:

**forest leaves → tree wētā → ruru (morepork)**

- The leaves come from a **producer**.
- The tree wētā is the **primary consumer** (it eats the producer).
- The ruru is the **secondary consumer** (it eats the primary consumer).

Most animals eat more than one thing, so food chains join up into a **food web**. In a food web, a change to one population affects others. For example, ruru and stoats both eat wētā, so they **compete**. If stoats are trapped, ruru may have more food.

**Energy is lost at every step.** Only a small fraction (roughly a tenth) of the energy at one level passes on to the next. The rest is used for movement and other life processes, or is lost as heat. This is why food chains rarely have more than four or five links, and why there are far fewer top predators than plants.

> **Watch out:** The arrow points **to** the eater. "Leaves → wētā" means the wētā eats the leaves.

## Biotic and abiotic factors

The size of a population depends on many factors.

| **Biotic** (living) factors | **Abiotic** (non-living) factors |
|---|---|
| Food supply | Temperature |
| Predators | Light |
| Competition for food, space or mates | Water and rainfall |
| Disease and parasites | Soil type and pH |
| | Wind, salt and oxygen levels |

### Example: a beech mast

Every few years, beech trees produce a huge crop of seed, called a **mast**.

1. With plenty of food, mouse and rat numbers boom.
2. With plenty of prey, stoat numbers rise.
3. When the seed runs out, the hungry rats and stoats turn to native birds, eggs and chicks.

One change in the food supply ripples right through the food web.

## Nutrient cycles

Atoms such as carbon and nitrogen are used over and over again. Decomposers are essential, because they return nutrients from dead material to the soil and air.

**The carbon cycle**
- **Photosynthesis** takes carbon dioxide out of the air.
- **Respiration** (by plants, animals and decomposers) and **combustion** (burning) put carbon dioxide back.
- Carbon is **stored** in forests (wood), soils, **oceans** (dissolved, and in shells) and fossil fuels. Cutting down and burning forests releases stored carbon as carbon dioxide.

**The nitrogen cycle** (simplified)
- About 78% of air is nitrogen gas, but most plants can't use it.
- **Nitrogen-fixing bacteria**, in soil and in lumps on the roots of clover, turn it into nitrates. Lightning makes a little too.
- Plants absorb **nitrates** through their roots and use them to make proteins. Animals get nitrogen by eating plants.
- Decomposers break down dead material and waste, and other bacteria turn it back into nitrates. Some bacteria return nitrogen gas to the air.

**The water cycle:** water evaporates from the sea and land, and from leaves (transpiration). It condenses into clouds and falls as rain.

## Maramataka: reading the environment

For many generations, Māori have closely observed patterns in the natural world and passed this knowledge on. One example is the **maramataka**, the Māori lunar calendar. (*Marama* means moon.)

- The maramataka follows the **phases of the moon** through each month. Each night has its own name, and some nights are known as better or worse for activities such as **planting, harvesting and fishing**.
- It also uses **tohu**, signs in the environment, such as stars rising, plants flowering, and the behaviour of birds and fish.
- There isn't just one maramataka. Different iwi and hapū have their own, suited to their local environment.
- The rising of Matariki (or Puanga, for some iwi) in mid-winter marks the Māori new year.

The moon also affects the **tides**, which matter for fishing and gathering kaimoana. Like science, the maramataka is built on careful observation of patterns repeated over many years.

The maramataka is closely linked to **kaitiakitanga**, the responsibility to care for the environment for future generations. One practice is a **rāhui**, a temporary restriction on taking resources from an area so they can recover.

## Protecting Aotearoa's ecosystems

New Zealand's native species evolved for millions of years with no land mammals except bats. Since people arrived, ecosystems have changed fast.

- **Introduced predators:** rats (kiore came with Polynesian voyagers; ship rats and Norway rats came with Europeans), stoats (released in the 1880s to control rabbits), cats, and possums (brought from Australia for a fur trade). They eat eggs, chicks, birds, lizards and insects. Possums also eat leaves, flowers and fruit.
- **Habitat loss:** large areas of forest have been cleared, and about 90% of wetlands have been drained.
- **Disease:** kauri dieback spreads in soil, so walkers clean their shoes at forest tracks.

**Conservation efforts:**
- **Predator Free 2050**: a national goal to remove possums, rats and mustelids (stoats, ferrets and weasels) from New Zealand by 2050.
- Trapping and pest control by the Department of Conservation, iwi and community groups.
- Predator-free islands, such as Tiritiri Matangi, and fenced sanctuaries, such as Zealandia in Wellington.
- Recovery programmes, such as Kākāpō Recovery, and Operation Nest Egg for kiwi.

### Exam tips

- Arrows in food chains point **to the eater** and show energy flow.
- When you predict a change in a food web, go step by step: who loses a predator, who loses food, and who has less competition?
- Name abiotic and biotic factors correctly: anything living, including disease, is biotic.
- In carbon cycle answers, use the process names: photosynthesis, respiration, combustion, decomposition.
- Describe the maramataka accurately and generally: a lunar calendar based on observation, guiding planting, harvesting and fishing, with different versions for different iwi.
