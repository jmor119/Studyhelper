The Sun rising and setting, the changing shape of the Moon, the seasons, and Matariki appearing in the dawn sky in midwinter all follow from how the Earth, Moon and Sun move. People have used these patterns for thousands of years to keep time, plant crops and navigate across oceans.

## Day, night, years and seasons

- **Day and night:** the Earth spins on its **axis** (an imaginary line through the North and South Poles) once about every **24 hours**. The half facing the Sun has day and the other half has night. The Earth spins from west to east, so the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west.
- **A year:** the Earth travels once around the Sun in its **orbit** every **365¼ days**. The extra quarter day is why we add a leap day (29 February) every four years.

**Seasons** happen because Earth's axis is **tilted** by about 23.5°, and it stays tilted the same way all year as Earth orbits the Sun.

- In **December**, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted **towards** the Sun. The Sun climbs higher in our sky, so its light is more concentrated, and days are longer. That's summer in New Zealand. The longest day is around 21–22 December.
- In **June**, the Southern Hemisphere is tilted **away** from the Sun. The Sun stays low, its light is spread out, and days are short. That's winter here. The shortest day is around 21 June.
- The Northern Hemisphere always has the opposite season.

> **Watch out:** Seasons are **not** caused by Earth's distance from the Sun. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January, during the Northern Hemisphere's winter.

## Phases of the Moon

The Moon doesn't make its own light. It **reflects sunlight**. At any moment, half of the Moon is lit by the Sun. As the Moon orbits the Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. These are the **phases** of the Moon.

| Phase | What we see |
|---|---|
| New moon | Nothing: the lit side faces away from us (the Moon is between Earth and Sun) |
| Waxing crescent | A thin sliver, growing |
| First quarter | Half the disc lit |
| Waxing gibbous | More than half, growing |
| **Full moon** | The whole disc: the Moon is on the far side of Earth from the Sun |
| Waning gibbous | More than half, shrinking |
| Last quarter | Half the disc lit |
| Waning crescent | A thin sliver, shrinking |

**Waxing** means the lit part is growing; **waning** means it is shrinking. One full cycle, from new moon to new moon, takes about **29.5 days**. The Moon spins exactly once for each trip around the Earth, so the same side always faces us.

> **Tip:** In New Zealand a waxing Moon is lit on the **left** and a waning Moon on the **right**. It's the opposite in the Northern Hemisphere, because we see the Moon "upside down" compared with them.

## Eclipses

An **eclipse** happens when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up exactly and one body casts a shadow on another.

- **Solar eclipse:** the **Moon** passes between the Sun and the Earth, and the Moon's shadow falls on part of the Earth. It can only happen at **new moon**. A total solar eclipse can only be seen from a narrow strip of the Earth. **Never look directly at the Sun**, even during an eclipse, because it can permanently damage your eyes.
- **Lunar eclipse:** the **Earth** passes between the Sun and the Moon, and Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It can only happen at **full moon**. It can be seen from anywhere on the night side of the Earth and is safe to watch. The Moon often turns a coppery red, because a little sunlight is bent through Earth's atmosphere onto it.

**Why isn't there an eclipse every month?** The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° compared with Earth's orbit around the Sun, so usually the Moon passes just above or below the line between the Sun and the Earth.

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, but it is also about 400 times further away, so the two look almost exactly the same size in our sky. That's why the Moon can just cover the Sun in a total solar eclipse.

## Matariki, the maramataka and navigation

**Matariki** is the Māori name for a cluster of stars also known as the Pleiades. For about a month, from late autumn, it can't be seen, because it is too close to the Sun in the sky. In midwinter (late June or July), it reappears low in the north-eastern sky just before dawn. For many iwi this rising marks the start of the **Māori New Year**. Some iwi, for example in Taranaki and Whanganui, mark the new year with the rising of the star Puanga (Rigel) instead.

Matariki is a time to **remember** people who have died since the last Matariki, to **celebrate** the present by gathering and sharing food, and to **look ahead** and plan for the coming year. Some people read how clearly the stars could be seen as a sign of what the coming season would be like. Many people today recognise nine named stars in Matariki, each linked to part of the natural world, such as Tupuānuku (food grown in the ground) and Waitī (fresh water). Since 2022 Matariki has been a public holiday. Its date changes each year because it is set using the maramataka.

The **maramataka** is the Māori lunar calendar. It is based on the phases of the Moon: each night of the Moon's cycle has its own name and character. Together with signs in the environment, such as stars, flowering plants and the behaviour of birds and fish, it guides the best times for planting, harvesting and fishing. Each iwi has its own maramataka, suited to its local environment.

**Navigation:** Polynesian voyagers, including the ancestors of Māori, sailed huge distances across the Pacific in waka hourua (double-hulled voyaging canoes). They used the points on the horizon where particular stars rise and set, together with ocean swells, winds, birds and clouds. The Southern Cross helps find south.

## The Solar System

The **Solar System** is the Sun and everything that orbits it:

- the **Sun**, a star, which holds about 99.8% of the Solar System's mass
- eight **planets**, which are large, round bodies that orbit the Sun
- **dwarf planets** such as Pluto, reclassified in 2006
- **moons**, which orbit planets
- the **asteroid belt** of rocky bodies between Mars and Jupiter, and **comets**, which are icy bodies with tails.

| Planet | Distance from Sun (AU) | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury | 0.39 | Smallest planet. No real atmosphere: about 430 °C by day and −180 °C at night |
| Venus | 0.72 | Hottest planet (over 450 °C). A thick CO₂ atmosphere traps heat |
| Earth | 1 | Liquid water and life |
| Mars | 1.5 | Cold and dusty, with a thin CO₂ atmosphere. Red from iron oxide (rust) |
| Jupiter | 5.2 | Largest planet; a gas giant with a storm bigger than Earth |
| Saturn | 9.5 | Gas giant with spectacular rings of ice and rock |
| Uranus | 19 | Ice giant, tipped over on its side |
| Neptune | 30 | Ice giant with the fastest winds |

The inner four are small and **rocky**. The outer four are **giants** made mostly of gas and ice.

**Distances in space:**

- One **astronomical unit (AU)** is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, about **150 million km**.
- A **light-year** is the **distance** light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion km. Light travels at about 300,000 km per second, so sunlight takes about 8 minutes to reach us. The nearest star after the Sun is about 4.2 light-years away.

Our **Moon** is about 384,000 km away. It has almost no atmosphere, so it has no weather to wear away its craters, and its temperature swings from over 100 °C in sunlight to around −130 °C at night.

> **Watch out:** A light-year measures **distance**, not time.

## Exam tips

- Day and night: Earth spinning on its axis (24 h). A year: one orbit of the Sun (365¼ days).
- Seasons are caused by the **tilt** of the axis, not distance. Explain using the height of the Sun, how concentrated the sunlight is, and day length.
- Moon phases: we see different amounts of the lit half as the Moon orbits. The cycle takes about 29.5 days.
- Solar eclipse: the Moon blocks the Sun (at new moon). Lunar eclipse: Earth's shadow falls on the Moon (at full moon).
- Matariki (the Pleiades) rises before dawn in midwinter and marks the Māori New Year for many iwi. The maramataka is the Māori lunar calendar.
- Planet order: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune. 1 AU ≈ 150 million km; a light-year is a distance.
