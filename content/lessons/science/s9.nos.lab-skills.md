A science lab has hot flames, glassware and chemicals, so it has its own rules. Knowing the rules, the equipment and how to use a Bunsen burner properly keeps you safe. It also makes your results more accurate, and most Year 9 tests include a few marks on it.

## Staying safe in the lab

A **hazard** is anything that could cause harm, such as a flame, hot glass, broken glass or a corrosive acid. Lab rules are there to stop hazards from hurting anyone.

| Rule | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Wear safety glasses when heating or using chemicals | Splashes of hot liquid or acid can damage your eyes |
| Tie back long hair; tuck in loose clothing | Hair and ties can catch fire in a Bunsen flame |
| Stand up when heating | You can step back quickly if something spills |
| No eating or drinking | Food can pick up harmful chemicals |
| Waft smells towards your nose | Breathing in strong fumes directly can hurt your nose and lungs |
| Tell the teacher straight away about spills, breakages and injuries | They can be cleaned up or treated safely |

If you get a burn, cool it under cold running water straight away (first-aid advice in NZ is 20 minutes) and tell your teacher. If a chemical splashes in your eye, rinse it with lots of water at the eyewash and get help.

Chemical bottles carry **hazard symbols**. A flame means **flammable** (catches fire easily), and a liquid eating into a hand and a surface means **corrosive** (damages skin and materials).

## Common lab equipment

| Equipment | What it's for |
|---|---|
| **Beaker** | Holding, mixing and heating liquids. Its scale only gives a rough volume. |
| **Conical flask** | Mixing and swirling liquids. The narrow neck stops them splashing out. |
| **Test tube** | Small amounts of chemicals for quick reactions |
| **Measuring cylinder** | Measuring the volume of a liquid accurately |
| **Tripod** | A stand that holds a gauze mat and beaker above a Bunsen burner |
| **Gauze mat** | Sits on the tripod, supports the beaker and spreads the heat evenly |
| **Heatproof mat** | Goes under the Bunsen burner to protect the bench |
| **Test-tube holder** | Holds a hot test tube so you don't touch it |

> **Watch out:** Hot glass looks exactly like cold glass. Leave heated equipment to cool, or pick it up with tongs.

## The Bunsen burner

A **Bunsen burner** burns gas to give a flame. Its main parts are the **base**, the **gas hose**, the **barrel** (the tube the flame comes out of) and the **collar**, which you turn to open or close the **air hole**.

**Lighting it safely:**
1. Put the Bunsen burner on a heatproof mat and connect the hose to the gas tap.
2. Close the air hole.
3. Light your match or lighter and hold it just above the top of the barrel.
4. Turn on the gas. A yellow flame appears.
5. Open the air hole when you need a hotter flame.

Lighting the match **before** you turn on the gas stops unburnt gas building up.

| | **Safety flame** | **Blue flame** |
|---|---|---|
| Air hole | Closed | Open |
| Colour | Yellow, wavy | Blue, with an inner cone; roars |
| How hot? | Cooler | Much hotter |
| Easy to see? | Yes | No, it's nearly invisible in daylight |
| Soot? | Leaves black soot on glassware | Clean |
| Use it for | Showing the burner is lit when you're not heating | Heating |

The hottest part of the blue flame is just above the tip of the inner blue cone. When you finish heating, close the air hole to go back to the safety flame. To turn it off, turn off the gas tap.

## Heating safely

**Heating a beaker:** stand the beaker on a gauze mat on a tripod, with the Bunsen burner underneath on a heatproof mat. Use the blue flame.

**Heating a test tube:**
- Fill it no more than about a third full.
- Hold it near the top with a **test-tube holder**, at an angle.
- Point the open end **away** from yourself and other people.
- Heat it gently and keep it moving in the flame, so the liquid doesn't suddenly boil and spit out.

**After heating:** turn off the gas, let things cool, and tell the teacher about any broken glass. Broken glass goes in the glass bin, not the rubbish bin.

> **Tip:** Exam questions often describe an unsafe student, such as one sitting down with loose hair and no glasses, pointing a test tube at a friend. Say what's wrong, how to fix it, **and why**.

## Measuring accurately

When water is in a narrow tube, its surface curves down in the middle. This curve is the **meniscus**. Read the volume from the **bottom of the meniscus**, with your eye **level** with it.

If you look from above or below, the reading will be wrong. This is called **parallax error**. Looking down from above makes the reading too high.

**Reading a scale:**
1. Find two labelled marks, e.g. 40 mL and 50 mL.
2. Count the spaces between them. If there are 5 spaces, each small mark is $10 \div 5 = 2$ mL.
3. Count up from the lower label to the bottom of the meniscus.

**Choosing equipment:** use a **measuring cylinder**, not a beaker, to measure volumes. Pick the smallest cylinder that holds the volume you need, because it has the finest scale. To measure 18 mL, a 25 mL cylinder beats a 100 mL one.

## Exam tips

- Learn the Bunsen order: **air hole closed → light the match → gas on**, then open the air hole to heat.
- Safety flame: **yellow, visible, cooler, sooty**. Blue flame: **hot, clean, hard to see**.
- For any safety rule, give the **reason** too ("tie hair back **so it can't catch fire**").
- Read from the **bottom of the meniscus at eye level**, and work out what each small mark is worth before you read a scale.
- Use the right word: a measuring **cylinder** measures, a **beaker** holds, a **conical flask** swirls.
