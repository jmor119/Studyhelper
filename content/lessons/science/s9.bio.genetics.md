Why do you look a bit like your parents, but not exactly like them? The answer is in your **DNA**, a set of instructions in almost every one of your cells. In this lesson you'll learn how DNA and genes work, why living things vary, and how genetic information passes from parents to their children.

## DNA, chromosomes and genes

**DNA** is the chemical that carries genetic information. It is found in the nucleus. DNA is a very long molecule shaped like a twisted ladder, called a **double helix**. To fit inside the nucleus, it is tightly coiled into **chromosomes**.

- Human body cells have **46 chromosomes**, arranged in **23 pairs**.
- One chromosome of each pair came from your mother, and the other came from your father.

A **gene** is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for making one protein. Each chromosome carries hundreds or thousands of genes. The instructions are written in a code of four chemical "letters", called bases: **A, T, C and G**. The order of the bases in a gene is the instruction.

> **Tip:** Think of DNA as a recipe book. Each chromosome is a chapter, each gene is one recipe, and the protein is the dish the recipe makes.

## Genes make proteins

Proteins do most of the work in your body. Your genes tell your cells which proteins to make.

| Type of protein | Job | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Enzymes** | Speed up chemical reactions | Amylase, which digests starch |
| **Hormones** (some) | Chemical messengers carried in the blood | Insulin |
| **Antibodies** | Help the body fight germs | Made by white blood cells |
| **Structural proteins** | Build body parts | Keratin (hair, nails), collagen (skin) |

Other proteins carry substances. Haemoglobin, for example, carries oxygen in red blood cells.

The proteins your cells make produce your characteristics, or **traits**:

**gene → protein → trait**

For example, the colour of your skin, hair and eyes depends on how much of a pigment called melanin your cells make, and enzymes are needed to make melanin.

## Genes plus environment

Your **phenotype** is all the characteristics you show: your height, your blood group, the colour of your hair. Most characteristics come from your **genes and your environment working together**.

- **Height:** your genes set a range, but your diet and health affect how tall you actually grow.
- **Skin colour:** genes set your natural skin colour, but sunlight can make it darker.
- **Plants:** seedlings from the same packet grow tall, thin and pale in dim light, but short and green in bright light.

**Identical twins** have exactly the same genes, so any differences between them come from their environment.

Some characteristics come from the environment alone, such as a scar, pierced ears or the language you speak. These are **acquired characteristics**. They aren't stored in your DNA, so you can't pass them on to your children.

> **Watch out:** Some characteristics are controlled by genes alone, such as your ABO blood group. Nothing you do can change it.

## Variation

**Variation** means the differences between individuals of the same species. There are two kinds:

| | Continuous variation | Discontinuous variation |
|---|---|---|
| What it looks like | Any value in a range | A few distinct groups, with nothing in between |
| Examples | Height, body mass, hand span, leaf length | ABO blood group; round or wrinkled pea seeds |
| Usually controlled by | Many genes, plus the environment | One gene (a **single-gene trait**), with little effect from the environment |
| Graph | Histogram (bars touch) | Bar chart (gaps between bars) |

For continuous variation, the histogram is often bell-shaped: most people are near the middle, and only a few are very short or very tall.

> **Tip:** Ask yourself, "Could I measure this with a ruler or scales?" If yes, it's probably continuous. If you'd sort it into groups, it's discontinuous.

## Inheritance

**Inheritance** is how genetic information passes from parents to their offspring.

In **sexual reproduction**, a sperm cell and an egg cell join (fertilisation). Each of these sex cells carries **23 chromosomes**, one from each pair. The fertilised egg has 23 + 23 = **46** chromosomes, so the baby gets half its genes from each parent.

- Every sperm and every egg carries a different mix of the parent's chromosomes, so brothers and sisters look different.
- **Identical twins** grow from one fertilised egg that splits in two, so they share the same genes.

In **asexual reproduction**, there is only one parent, and the offspring are **clones** with identical genes. Examples are strawberry plants sending out runners, potatoes sprouting from tubers, and bacteria splitting in two.

Variation matters for survival. If a new disease arrives, a group of clones could all be wiped out. In a varied population, some individuals may have genes that help them survive and reproduce.

## Exam tips

- Learn the size order: **gene → chromosome → nucleus → cell**.
- When you explain a characteristic, decide whether it comes from genes, the environment or both. Most come from both.
- "Continuous = measure it; discontinuous = sort it into groups." Then match the graph: histogram for continuous, bar chart for discontinuous.
- In inheritance questions, remember: sex cells have half the chromosomes, so fertilisation restores the full number.
- An acquired characteristic, such as fitness or a scar, is **never** inherited.
