Hardly anything around you is a pure element. Water, salt, sugar, air and sea water are all made of elements combined in different ways. This lesson shows you how to tell compounds from mixtures, read chemical formulas, and separate mixtures back into their parts.

## Elements, compounds and mixtures

- An **element** contains only one type of atom, e.g. oxygen or iron.
- A **compound** contains two or more different elements **chemically joined** (bonded) together in a fixed ratio, e.g. water, where there are always 2 hydrogen atoms for every 1 oxygen atom.
- A **mixture** contains two or more substances that are **not chemically joined**, e.g. air or salty water.

A **molecule** is a particle made of two or more atoms joined together. The atoms can be the same (an oxygen molecule, O₂, is an element) or different (a water molecule, H₂O, is a compound).

| | Compound | Mixture |
|---|---|---|
| Are the parts joined? | Yes, chemically bonded | No |
| Amounts | Always a fixed ratio | Can be any amounts |
| Properties | New, different properties | Each part keeps its own properties |
| Separating | Only by a chemical reaction | By physical methods, e.g. filtering or a magnet |

**Example:** Iron filings and sulfur powder stirred together make a mixture. A magnet pulls the iron out. Heat them and they react to make a new compound, iron sulfide. Now the magnet can't pull the iron out, because the iron atoms are chemically joined to sulfur atoms.

> **Tip:** In particle diagrams, look at each particle. Joined atoms of *different* kinds mean a compound. Two or more *different kinds* of particle in the same box mean a mixture.

## Reading formulas

A **formula** shows which elements are in a substance and how many atoms of each there are.

- Each symbol is one element (a capital letter starts a new symbol).
- A small number written after a symbol (a subscript) tells you how many of **that** atom there are. No number means 1.

| Formula | What it shows | Total atoms |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | 2 H, 1 O | 3 |
| CO₂ | 1 C, 2 O | 3 |
| CH₄ (methane) | 1 C, 4 H | 5 |
| C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) | 6 C, 12 H, 6 O | 24 |

### Example

How many atoms are in one molecule of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄?

H: 2, S: 1, O: 4, so the total is $2 + 1 + 4 = 7$ atoms.

> **Watch out:** A number after a bracket multiplies everything inside the bracket. Ca(OH)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H.

## Molecules and ionic lattices

Compounds come in two main types.

**Molecular substances** are made of separate molecules, such as water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂) and sugar. They usually form between non-metals. The forces *between* the molecules are weak, so molecular substances usually have **low melting and boiling points**, and many are liquids or gases at room temperature. They don't conduct electricity.

**Ionic compounds**, such as salts, usually form when a metal combines with a non-metal. The atoms become **ions**: particles with an electric charge. In sodium chloride (table salt), positive sodium ions and negative chloride ions are packed in a huge, regular, repeating 3D pattern called a **lattice**. The opposite charges attract strongly, so ionic compounds:

- have **high melting points** (sodium chloride melts at 801 °C)
- form hard but brittle crystals
- often dissolve in water
- conduct electricity when melted or dissolved, because the ions can then move. They don't conduct as solids.

> **Watch out:** There are no "NaCl molecules". The formula NaCl tells you the *ratio* of ions in the lattice: one sodium for every chloride.

## Pure substances and solutions

In chemistry, a **pure substance** contains only one substance: one element or one compound. Distilled water is pure. "Pure orange juice" isn't, because it's a mixture of water, sugars, acids and more. Pure substances melt and boil at fixed temperatures. Mixtures melt and boil over a range of temperatures.

A **solution** is a mixture made when one substance dissolves in another:

- the **solute** is the substance that dissolves (e.g. sugar)
- the **solvent** is the liquid it dissolves in (e.g. water)
- the solution is the mixture you get (sugar solution).

A substance that dissolves is **soluble**; one that doesn't (like sand) is **insoluble**. **Solubility** is the mass of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature, often given in grams per 100 g of water. When no more solute will dissolve, the solution is **saturated**. Most solids are more soluble in hotter water.

Dissolving is not "disappearing". The solute particles spread out between the solvent particles, and the mass is kept:

$$\text{mass of solution} = \text{mass of solute} + \text{mass of solvent}$$

## Separating mixtures

Because the parts of a mixture aren't joined, physical methods can separate them.

| Method | Separates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Filtration** | an insoluble solid from a liquid | sand from water. The sand stays on the filter paper (the residue); the water passes through (the filtrate). |
| **Evaporation** | a dissolved solid from its solution | salt crystals from salt water. Heat until the water evaporates. |
| **Distillation** | a liquid from a solution, or liquids with different boiling points | pure water from sea water. The water boils, the vapour is cooled in a condenser, and it drips out as pure water. |
| **Chromatography** | dissolved substances, such as the dyes in an ink | Different dyes travel different distances up the paper with the solvent. |
| **Magnet** | a magnetic metal from other substances | iron filings from sulfur |

> **Tip:** In chromatography, draw the start line in **pencil**. Pencil doesn't dissolve, but pen ink would run up the paper and mix with your results.

## Exam tips

- Element: one type of atom. Compound: different elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio. Mixture: substances not joined.
- To count atoms, a subscript applies only to the symbol just before it. A number after a bracket multiplies everything inside.
- Molecular substances: low melting points and no conduction. Ionic lattices: high melting points, and they conduct when melted or dissolved.
- Solute + solvent → solution. Learn solubility and saturated too.
- Choose a separation method by asking: is the solid dissolved or not? Do I want the solid or the liquid?
