Every living thing, from a bacterium to a giant kauri tree, is made of **cells**. Cells are the building blocks of life, and most are far too small to see without a microscope. In this lesson you'll learn to use a microscope, work out magnification, and see what each part of a cell does.

## Using a light microscope

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| Eyepiece lens | The lens you look through (usually ×10) |
| Objective lenses | Lenses just above the slide (often ×4, ×10 and ×40) |
| Stage | Holds the slide, with clips |
| Coarse focus | Big movements, to find the image |
| Fine focus | Small movements, to make the image sharp |
| Light source and diaphragm | Shine light up through the slide and control how much |

**How to focus:**

1. Put the slide on the stage, with the lowest-power objective lens in position.
2. Watching from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the lens close to the slide.
3. Looking through the eyepiece, move the lens and slide apart with the coarse focus until the image appears.
4. Sharpen the image with the fine focus. At higher power, use only the fine focus.

To make a slide, use a very thin layer of cells and add a **stain** so the parts show up: iodine for onion cells, or methylene blue for cheek cells. Lower the cover slip at an angle to avoid trapping air bubbles.

> **Watch out:** Never move the lens towards the slide while looking through the eyepiece. You could crack the slide or damage the lens.

## Calculating magnification

There are two ways to work out magnification.

**1. From the lenses:**

$$\text{total magnification} = \text{eyepiece} \times \text{objective}$$

A ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives $10 \times 40 = 400$, so the magnification is ×400.

**2. From sizes**, for a drawing or photo:

$$\text{magnification} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{actual size}}$$

Both sizes must be in the **same units**. Cells are measured in **micrometres (µm)**, and 1 mm = 1000 µm.

### Example

In a photo, a cheek cell is 30 mm wide. The real cell is 60 µm wide. What is the magnification?

1. Convert to the same units: 30 mm = 30 000 µm.
2. Divide: $30\,000 \div 60 = 500$.

The magnification is **×500**.

To find the real size, rearrange the formula: **actual size = image size ÷ magnification**.

> **Tip:** Magnification has no units. Write it as ×500, not 500 mm.

## Parts of a cell

Each part of a cell (an **organelle**) has a job.

| Structure | Job | Plant | Animal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nucleus** | Contains DNA and controls the cell's activities | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Cell membrane** | Controls what enters and leaves the cell | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Cytoplasm** | Jelly-like liquid where many chemical reactions happen | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Mitochondria** | Release energy from glucose by respiration | ✓ | ✓ |
| **Cell wall** | Made of cellulose; supports the cell and gives it a fixed shape | ✓ | ✗ |
| **Chloroplasts** | Contain green chlorophyll; absorb light for photosynthesis | ✓ | ✗ |
| **Vacuole** (large, permanent) | Holds cell sap and keeps the cell firm | ✓ | ✗ |

> **Watch out:** Plant cells have a cell wall **and** a cell membrane. The wall is outside the membrane. It doesn't replace it.

> **Tip:** Mitochondria *release* energy from glucose. They don't *make* energy, because energy can't be created.

## Plant and animal cells compared

Plant and animal cells share a nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm and mitochondria. Only plant cells have a cell wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent vacuole.

These differences explain what you see under the microscope:

- Plant cells have a regular, box-like shape because the rigid cell wall holds them in place. Animal cells, such as cheek cells, have irregular shapes.
- Chloroplasts appear only in the green parts of plants. Root cells grow underground with no light, so they have no chloroplasts.
- Animal cells may have small, temporary vacuoles, but never one large permanent vacuole.

## From cells to organisms

Many cells are **specialised**: their shape and features suit one job. A red blood cell has no nucleus, leaving more room to carry oxygen. A root hair cell has a long, thin extension that gives a large surface area for absorbing water.

Cells build up in **levels of organisation**:

| Level | Meaning | Human example | Plant example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell | The basic unit of life | Muscle cell | Palisade cell |
| **Tissue** | A group of similar cells doing one job | Muscle tissue | Xylem |
| **Organ** | Several tissues working together | Heart | Leaf |
| **Organ system** | Several organs working together | Circulatory system | Shoot system |
| Organism | A whole living thing | Human | Kōwhai tree |

## Exam tips

- Learn both magnification formulas, and always convert to the same units before dividing (1 mm = 1000 µm).
- To find the real size, use actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
- For a scientific drawing, use a sharp pencil, clear outlines with no shading, and label lines drawn with a ruler.
- When you compare cells, say which cell has the structure **and** which doesn't. "Plant cells have a cell wall but animal cells don't" earns the mark. "Plant cells have a cell wall" on its own may not.
- If you're asked why a cell has a feature, link the structure to its job. For example: lots of mitochondria → more respiration → more energy released.
