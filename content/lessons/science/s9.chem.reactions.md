Toasting bread, a rusty gate, a barbecue and your own body turning food into energy are all **chemical reactions**: changes that make new substances. This lesson shows you how to recognise a reaction, write it as a word equation, and describe the energy it gives out or takes in.

## Physical and chemical changes

A **physical change** doesn't make any new substance. The substance is still the same stuff, just in a different form, and the change is often easy to reverse. Examples: melting butter, freezing water, dissolving sugar, cutting paper.

A **chemical change** (a chemical reaction) makes one or more **new substances**, and it is usually hard to reverse. Examples: burning, rusting, cooking an egg, baking a cake, digesting food.

In a chemical reaction the atoms are **rearranged**: bonds between atoms break and new ones form. No atoms are created or destroyed, so the total mass stays the same. This is called **conservation of mass**.

> **Watch out:** Dissolving is a *physical* change. The sugar hasn't become something new, and you can get it back by evaporating the water.

## Signs of a chemical reaction

You can't see atoms rearranging, but these clues tell you a reaction is probably happening:

- **a gas is given off**: bubbles or fizzing
- **a colour change**: e.g. green copper carbonate turning black when heated
- **a temperature change**: the mixture gets hotter or colder
- **a precipitate forms**: two clear solutions mix and a solid appears, making the mixture cloudy. Blue copper sulfate solution + sodium hydroxide solution gives a pale blue solid.
- **light or sound**: e.g. a flame, a flash or a pop.

### Testing for gases

| Gas | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | Hold a lit splint at the mouth of the test tube | Squeaky pop |
| Oxygen | Put a glowing splint into the gas | The splint relights |
| Carbon dioxide | Bubble the gas through limewater | The limewater turns milky (cloudy) |

> **Watch out:** One sign on its own isn't proof. Boiling water makes bubbles, but that is a physical change.

## Word equations

A **word equation** shows what happens in a reaction:

$$\text{reactants} \rightarrow \text{products}$$

- **Reactants** are the substances you start with (on the left).
- **Products** are the new substances made (on the right).
- "+" means "and"; the arrow means "react to make".

### Example

Magnesium ribbon burns in air with a dazzling white light and leaves a white powder, magnesium oxide.

magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide

More examples:

- zinc + hydrochloric acid → zinc chloride + hydrogen
- copper carbonate → copper oxide + carbon dioxide (when heated)

Mass is conserved. If 2.4 g of magnesium combines with 1.6 g of oxygen, 4.0 g of magnesium oxide forms.

> **Tip:** When a word equation question describes a reaction, underline what you start with (the reactants) and circle what is made (the products). Oxygen from the air counts as a reactant.

## Combustion and rusting

**Combustion** (burning) is a reaction between a fuel and **oxygen** that releases heat and light. Many fuels, such as natural gas (methane), LPG and petrol, contain carbon and hydrogen. When they burn with plenty of oxygen:

fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

For example, methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.

A fire needs three things: **fuel, heat and oxygen** (the fire triangle). Take any one away and the fire goes out.

With too little oxygen, **incomplete combustion** happens. It makes soot (carbon) and carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas you can't see or smell. A Bunsen burner with its air hole closed burns with a yellow, sooty flame for this reason.

**Oxidation** is a reaction where a substance combines with oxygen. Burning is fast oxidation. **Rusting** is slow oxidation of iron:

iron + oxygen + water → rust (hydrated iron oxide)

Iron needs **both oxygen and water** to rust. Salt speeds rusting up, which is why cars and gates rust faster near the sea. Ways to prevent rust include painting, oiling or greasing, and galvanising (coating the iron with zinc). All of these keep the oxygen and water away.

## Energy changes

Every reaction involves an energy change.

- An **exothermic** reaction **gives out** heat energy to the surroundings, so the temperature goes **up**. Examples: combustion, respiration, neutralisation, and the iron in a hand warmer oxidising.
- An **endothermic** reaction **takes in** energy from the surroundings, so the temperature goes **down**, or energy must keep being supplied. Examples: photosynthesis (which takes in light energy), citric acid reacting with baking soda (the fizzy, cool feeling of sherbet), and copper carbonate breaking down, which only continues while it is heated.

> **Tip:** *Ex*othermic = heat *ex*its. *En*dothermic = heat *en*ters.

### Reactions in living things

**Respiration** happens in the cells of all living things, mainly in the mitochondria. It releases the energy stored in glucose, so it is exothermic:

glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water (+ energy)

**Photosynthesis** happens in the chloroplasts of plants. It uses light energy, so it is endothermic:

carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen (light energy is taken in)

Notice that one equation is the reverse of the other. Yeast does another reaction, fermentation, which makes carbon dioxide gas. That's what makes bread dough rise.

## Exam tips

- Chemical change = a new substance is made. Physical change = no new substance (melting, dissolving, changing shape).
- Learn the five signs: gas, colour change, temperature change, precipitate, light or sound. Learn the three gas tests too.
- Word equations: reactants → products. Don't forget oxygen from the air in burning and rusting.
- Combustion: fuel + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. Rusting: iron + oxygen + water → rust.
- Exothermic gives out heat (the temperature rises); endothermic takes in heat (the temperature falls).
- Respiration and photosynthesis are reverse reactions: learn both word equations.
- Mass is conserved, but a gas escaping from an open container makes it look as if mass was lost.
