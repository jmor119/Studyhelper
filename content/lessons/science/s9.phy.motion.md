Everything around you is moving: cars, netballs, the wind, even the ground under you as the Earth spins. Physics describes motion with a few key ideas: **speed**, **velocity** and **acceleration**. This topic shows you how to calculate them and how to read motion graphs.

## Speed

**Speed** is how far something travels in a certain time.

$$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \qquad v = \frac{d}{t}$$

The SI unit of speed is metres per second (**m/s**). Distance is in metres (m) and time is in seconds (s). Kilometres per hour (km/h) is also common, for example on road signs.

You can rearrange the formula:
- distance = speed × time ($d = vt$)
- time = distance ÷ speed ($t = \frac{d}{v}$)

### Example

Aroha runs 200 m in 40 s. What is her average speed?

$v = \dfrac{d}{t} = \dfrac{200}{40} = 5$ m/s

> **Tip:** Write the formula, put in the numbers, then give the answer **with units**. Most marking schemes give a mark for each step.

## Changing units

The units have to match. For an answer in m/s, use metres and seconds. For km/h, use kilometres and hours.

| To change | Do this |
|---|---|
| minutes → seconds | × 60 |
| hours → seconds | × 3,600 |
| km → m | × 1,000 |
| km/h → m/s | ÷ 3.6 |
| m/s → km/h | × 3.6 |

Why 3.6? 1 km/h means 1,000 m in 3,600 s, and $1000 \div 3600 = \frac{1}{3.6}$ m/s.

### Example

A bus travels 54 km/h. What is that in m/s?

$54 \div 3.6 = 15$ m/s

### Example

Mere walks 1,800 m in 20 minutes. What is her average speed in m/s?

Time in seconds: $20 \times 60 = 1200$ s, so $v = \dfrac{1800}{1200} = 1.5$ m/s.

## Average speed, instantaneous speed and velocity

**Average speed** is the total distance divided by the total time for a whole journey. **Instantaneous speed** is the speed at one particular moment. That's what a car's speedometer shows.

On a bus trip from Hamilton to Auckland, the bus might stop at lights (0 km/h) and cruise on the motorway (100 km/h). Its average speed for the whole trip is somewhere in between.

**Velocity** is speed in a particular **direction**, for example 15 m/s north. Two cars driving at 50 km/h in opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities.

> **Watch out:** A car going round a roundabout at a steady 30 km/h has a constant speed, but its velocity is changing because its direction keeps changing.

## Acceleration

**Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity: how much the velocity changes each second.

$$a = \frac{\Delta v}{t} = \frac{\text{final velocity} - \text{starting velocity}}{\text{time taken}}$$

$\Delta v$ (said "delta v") means "change in velocity". Acceleration is measured in metres per second per second (**m/s²**). An acceleration of 3 m/s² means the velocity goes up by 3 m/s every second.

### Example

A car speeds up from 10 m/s to 25 m/s in 5 s.

$\Delta v = 25 - 10 = 15$ m/s, so $a = \dfrac{15}{5} = 3$ m/s².

If an object **slows down**, the change in velocity is negative, so the acceleration is negative. This is often called **deceleration**. A car slowing from 20 m/s to 0 in 4 s has $a = \frac{0 - 20}{4} = -5$ m/s².

Because velocity includes direction, changing direction is also a kind of acceleration, even at a steady speed.

## Motion graphs

**Distance–time graphs** (distance up the side, time along the bottom):
- **Straight sloping line**: constant speed. The steeper the line, the faster.
- **Horizontal line**: stopped (stationary). The distance isn't changing.
- **Curve getting steeper**: speeding up. **Curve getting flatter**: slowing down.
- Speed = the gradient (slope) = distance gone ÷ time taken for that section.

**Speed–time** (or **velocity–time**) **graphs**:
- **Horizontal line**: constant speed. (It is **not** stopped, unless the line is on zero.)
- **Line sloping up**: speeding up (accelerating). **Line sloping down**: slowing down.
- Acceleration = the gradient = change in speed ÷ time taken.

> **Watch out:** A flat line means something completely different on the two graphs. Always read the axis labels first.

## Exam tips

- Learn the three formulas: $v = \frac{d}{t}$, $d = vt$, $t = \frac{d}{v}$. Acceleration is $a = \frac{\Delta v}{t}$.
- Check the units **before** you calculate: m and s for m/s; km and h for km/h. ÷ 3.6 changes km/h to m/s.
- Show your working: formula → numbers → answer with units (m/s or m/s²).
- "From rest" means starting velocity = 0. Slowing down gives a negative acceleration.
- On graphs, read the **axis labels** first. A flat line means **stopped** on a distance–time graph but **constant speed** on a speed–time graph.
