"For every" is one of the most useful ideas in maths. Recipes, paint mixes, team sizes and prices all use it. In this lesson you will scale amounts up and down, use fractions to describe parts of a whole, and work with rates.

## "For every"

"For every 3 red beads there are 7 blue beads" tells you how two amounts are related. It does **not** tell you how many beads there are. It tells you that the beads come in **groups** of 3 red and 7 blue.

To **scale** up, you make more groups. Every amount is multiplied by the same number. When amounts grow by multiplying like this, we say they are in **proportion**. The relationship is **multiplicative**.

### Example

There are 3 red balls for every 7 blue balls. There are 18 red balls. How many balls are there altogether?

- How many groups? $18 \div 3 = 6$ groups.
- Each group has $3 + 7 = 10$ balls.
- Altogether: $6 \times 10 = 60$ balls.

So $\frac{3}{10}$ of the beads are red and $\frac{7}{10}$ are blue. The two fractions add up to 1 whole.

> **Watch out:** $\frac{3}{7}$ is **not** the fraction of beads that are red. $\frac{3}{7}$ compares the red beads with the blue beads. A fraction of the whole always has the whole on the bottom.

### Example

$\frac{2}{5}$ of a class walk to school, and the rest come by bus. So $\frac{3}{5}$ come by bus: for every 2 who walk, 3 come by bus. If 12 students walk, there are $12 \div 2 = 6$ groups, so $6 \times 3 = 18$ come by bus.

## Scaling recipes

A recipe is a "for every" relationship too. To make more or less, multiply or divide **every** ingredient by the same number.

### Example

A pancake recipe for 4 people uses 160 g of flour and 240 mL of milk.

For 12 people: 12 is $3 \times 4$, so multiply everything by 3. That is 480 g of flour and 720 mL of milk.

For 6 people: 6 isn't a whole number of times 4. Go to 2 people first (divide by 2), then to 6 people (multiply by 3).

| People | 4 | 2 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flour (g) | 160 | 80 | 240 |
| Milk (mL) | 240 | 120 | 360 |

> **Tip:** Find an amount that is easy to get to, like half, or the amount for 1, then build up from there.

> **Watch out:** Don't add the same amount to each ingredient. Scaling is multiplying.

## Rates

A **rate** compares two different kinds of amount, like the cost of each item or the kilometres travelled each hour. To scale a rate, it often helps to find the amount for **one** first.

### Example

5 muesli bars cost \$6. How much do 8 cost?

- One bar: $6 \div 5 = 1.20$, so \$1.20.
- Eight bars: $8 \times 1.20 = 9.60$, so \$9.60.

### Example

A tap fills 18 L in 3 minutes. How long does it take to put 42 L in a tank?

- In 1 minute: $18 \div 3 = 6$ L.
- $42 \div 6 = 7$, so it takes 7 minutes.

## Putting it together

- Look for the groups. What goes with what?
- Multiply or divide every amount by the same number. Never add the same amount to each.
- Part to part ("3 red for every 7 blue") is different from part to whole ("3 out of every 10 are red").
- If the scale isn't a whole number, go through 1, or through a number that divides both, first.
- Check that your answer makes sense. More people need more of every ingredient.

### Example

For every 2 students who walk to school, 3 come by bus and 1 bikes. 12 students bike. How many students are there altogether?

Each group has $2 + 3 + 1 = 6$ students, including 1 who bikes. 12 bike, so there are 12 groups, and $12 \times 6 = 72$ students altogether.
