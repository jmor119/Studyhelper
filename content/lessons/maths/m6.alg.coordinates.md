Coordinates tell you exactly where something is on a grid. Maps, games and graphs all use them. In this lesson you will read and plot points, find points on the axes, and plot a growing pattern.

## The coordinate plane

Take two number lines. Put one across and one going up, so they cross at a right angle. Lines that cross like this are **perpendicular**. They meet at 0.

This makes a **coordinate plane**. You might also hear it called an **XY graph**.

- The number line going across is the **x-axis**.
- The number line going up is the **y-axis**.
- The point where they meet is the **origin**. Its coordinates are $(0, 0)$.

## Ordered pairs

We find a point using two numbers called its **coordinates**. They are written as an **ordered pair**, $(x, y)$.

- The first number, $x$, tells you how far to go **across**.
- The second number, $y$, tells you how far to go **up**.

### Example

To **plot** the point $P(4, 2)$:

1. Start at the origin, $(0, 0)$.
2. Go 4 across.
3. Then go 2 up. Mark the point.

## Points on the axes

If one coordinate is 0, the point sits on an axis.

- $(0, 3)$ is 0 across and 3 up, so it is **on the y-axis**.
- $(4, 0)$ is 4 across and 0 up, so it is **on the x-axis**.
- $(0, 0)$ is the origin, where the axes meet.

## Plotting a growing pattern

You can plot a growing pattern from its table of values. The **position** is the x-coordinate and the **term** is the y-coordinate.

Rule: start with 1 and add 2 each time.

| Position ($x$) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Term ($y$) | 1 | 3 | 5 | 7 |

The points are $(1, 1)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 5)$ and $(4, 7)$.

## Putting it together

- Across first ($x$), then up ($y$).
- A 0 in a coordinate means the point is on an axis.
- **Moving** right or left changes $x$. Moving up or down changes $y$.
- When you plot a pattern, the position is $x$ and the term is $y$.

### Example

Start at $(2, 5)$. Move 3 right and 2 down.

- $x$: $2 + 3 = 5$
- $y$: $5 - 2 = 3$

You finish at $(5, 3)$.

### Example

Three corners of a rectangle are $(1, 1)$, $(6, 1)$ and $(6, 4)$. The fourth corner lines up with $(1, 1)$ going up, and with $(6, 4)$ going across. So it is $(1, 4)$.
