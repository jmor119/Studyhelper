A good graph lets people see the story in your data at a glance. In this lesson you'll learn how to plan an investigation, which graph suits which kind of data, and what every good graph needs.

## Statistical questions

A **statistical question** is one you answer by collecting data, where the answers **vary**.

- "How many hours of sleep do Year 7 students usually get?" is a statistical question. Different students give different answers.
- "How many hours did I sleep last night?" is not. It has just one answer.

To answer a statistical question, **plan** first:

1. Decide what to count or measure (the variable).
2. Decide who to ask, or what to measure.
3. Decide how to record the results, such as a tally chart or a table.

### Example

Question: "Are our feet the same length?" Plan: measure each student's left foot and right foot to the nearest centimetre, and record both lengths in a table.

## What makes a good graph

Every good graph has:

- a **title** that says what the graph shows
- **axis labels**, with units such as °C or cm
- a sensible **scale** that goes up in equal steps (bar graphs start at 0)
- a **key** if there is more than one group
- information about **who the data came from**, such as "Room 9 students, Term 1"

| Data | Best display |
|---|---|
| One categorical variable | Bar graph (or dot plot) |
| One numerical variable | Dot plot |
| Two categorical variables | Clustered or stacked bar graph |
| Data collected over time | Time-series graph |

Look out for **outliers**: values far away from the rest. Check that they are real, and try to explain them. A student who lives on a farm might take 40 minutes to get to school.
