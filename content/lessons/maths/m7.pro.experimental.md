Some probabilities can't be worked out just by thinking. What is the chance that a drawing pin lands point up, or that a water-bottle flip lands upright? To find out, you run an experiment. In this lesson you'll learn how to estimate probabilities from experiments, and why more trials give better estimates.

## Experiments and relative frequency

In an **experiment** you repeat something many times and record what happens. Each go is called a **trial**.

The **experimental probability** of an outcome is also called its **relative frequency** or **estimated probability**:

$$\text{experimental probability} = \frac{\text{number of times the outcome happens}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$

### Example

Tama dropped a drawing pin 50 times. It landed point up 21 times.

$P(\text{point up}) \approx \frac{21}{50} = \frac{42}{100} = 0.42$, or 42%.

The $\approx$ sign means "is about". Another experiment would probably give a slightly different answer.

## Using a probability to predict

You can use a probability to predict roughly how many times something will happen:

$$\text{expected number} = \text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$

### Examples

- **From theory:** a fair die is rolled 60 times. You would expect about $\frac{1}{6} \times 60 = 10$ sixes.
- **From an experiment:** a bottle landed upright 9 times in 30 flips. In 300 flips (10 times as many), you would expect it to land upright about $9 \times 10 = 90$ times.

> **Tip:** Say "**about** 10 sixes". The real result will usually be close to the expected number, but not exactly the same.

## Experiment versus theory

Results **vary**. Three groups each tossed a coin 30 times and got 13, 17 and 15 heads. None of them is wrong: that is just how chance works.

When you compare an experiment with the theory, do at least 30 trials. The results may still differ from the theory because:

- of chance: each trial is random
- there weren't enough trials
- the object might not be fair, like a bent coin or a weighted die
- results were recorded wrongly

> **Watch out:** A coin has no memory. After five heads in a row, the chance of heads on the next toss is still $\frac{1}{2}$.

### Example

After 10 tosses, Mia had 7 heads (0.7). After 200 tosses, she had 101 heads (0.505), which is very close to the theoretical $\frac{1}{2}$.

**Combining results** gives more trials, so the estimate is more reliable. Four groups that each spin a spinner 25 times can combine their results into 100 spins.

## When you can only experiment

For a drawing pin, a paper cup or a **weighted** die (one that has been made to land on some numbers more often), the outcomes are not equally likely. Theory can't tell you the probabilities, so the only way to find out is to experiment, with lots of trials.

### Example

Hemi rolled a die 120 times.

| Number | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 15 | 16 | 14 | 17 | 15 | 43 |

For a fair die, each number would come up about $\frac{1}{6} \times 120 = 20$ times. Most results are close to 20, but 6 came up 43 times. That is strong evidence that the die is weighted towards 6. Based on these results, $P(6) \approx \frac{43}{120}$, which is more than a third.

> **Tip:** Small differences from the expected number are normal. Look for results that are a long way off.
