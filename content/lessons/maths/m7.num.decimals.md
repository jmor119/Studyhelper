Decimals are everywhere: race times, prices, lengths and weights. In this lesson you will add and subtract decimals, multiply and divide by powers of 10, multiply decimals by whole numbers, turn fractions into decimals, and round.

## Place value and adding decimals

A **decimal** uses place value to show parts of a whole. Each **decimal place** is 10 times smaller than the place to its left.

To add or subtract decimals, **line up the decimal points**. Then tenths are under tenths, and hundredths are under hundredths. Fill any empty places with zeros.

### Example
$5.3 + 2.45$: write 5.3 as 5.30.

$$\begin{array}{r} 5.30 \\ +\; 2.45 \\ \hline 7.75 \end{array}$$

$8 - 3.26$: write 8 as 8.00, then $8.00 - 3.26 = 4.74$.

## Multiplying and dividing by powers of 10

10, 100 and 1000 are **powers of 10**: $10^1 = 10$, $10^2 = 100$ and $10^3 = 1000$.

When you multiply by 10, every digit moves one place to the **left**, so the number gets 10 times bigger.

When you divide by 10, every digit moves one place to the **right**. Multiplying or dividing by 100 moves the digits two places, and by 1000 moves them three places. Use zeros to fill empty places.

### Examples
- $3.45 \times 100 = 345$
- $6.2 \div 100 = 0.062$
- $0.08 \times 10^3 = 0.08 \times 1000 = 80$

## Multiplying decimals by whole numbers

Use a number fact and place value. $0.7$ is 7 tenths, so $0.7 \times 5$ is $7 \times 5 = 35$ tenths, which is $3.5$.

The same fact helps with $0.7 \times 50$. It is 10 times bigger than $0.7 \times 5$, so it is $35$.

### Example
$2.6 \times 4$: split 2.6 into $2 + 0.6$.
- $2 \times 4 = 8$
- $0.6 \times 4 = 2.4$
- $8 + 2.4 = 10.4$

> **Tip:** Estimate to check. $2.6 \times 4$ is a bit less than $3 \times 4 = 12$, so 10.4 makes sense.

## Fractions to decimals

A fraction is a division. $\frac{3}{8}$ means $3 \div 8$. Write 3 as 3.000 and use short division:

$3 \div 8 = 0.375$

The division stops, so 0.375 is a **terminating decimal**.

Some divisions never stop. $1 \div 3 = 0.333\ldots$ and the 3 keeps repeating forever. This is a **recurring decimal**. You can write it with a dot over the repeating digit: $0.\dot{3}$.

### Examples
- $\frac{5}{16} = 5 \div 16 = 0.3125$ (terminating)
- $\frac{2}{3} = 0.666\ldots$ (recurring)
- $\frac{7}{3} = 2.333\ldots$ (recurring)
- $\frac{1}{7} = 0.142857142857\ldots$ (recurring: the block 142857 repeats)

## Rounding decimals

To **round** to the nearest tenth, look at the hundredths digit. If it is 5 or more, round up. If it is 4 or less, round down.

### Examples
- 3.47 to the nearest tenth: the hundredths digit is 7, so round up to **3.5**.
- 2.346 to the nearest hundredth: the thousandths digit is 6, so round up to **2.35**.
- 12.48 to the nearest whole number: the tenths digit is 4, so round down to **12**.
- $\frac{2}{3} = 0.666\ldots$ to 2 decimal places is **0.67**.

> **Watch out:** 5.96 to the nearest tenth is 6.0. Keep the zero to show you rounded to one decimal place.

**Putting it together:** line up decimal points to add and subtract, count places when you multiply or divide by 10, 100 or 1000, and estimate to check that your answer makes sense.
