Probability measures how likely something is to happen. When all the outcomes are equally likely, like rolling a fair die, you can work the probability out exactly. The key skill is listing every possible outcome without missing any or counting any twice.

## Probability basics

- An **outcome** is one possible result, e.g. rolling a 4.
- The **sample space** is the list of *all* possible outcomes: for a die, {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
- An **event** is a group of outcomes you're interested in, e.g. "rolling an even number" = {2, 4, 6}.

When all outcomes are **equally likely**:

$$P(\text{event}) = \frac{\text{number of outcomes in the event}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$

So $P(\text{even}) = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$.

Probabilities go from **0** (impossible) to **1** (certain). You can write them as fractions, decimals or percentages: $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25 = 25\%$.

The probability that an event does **not** happen is

$$P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$$

because the event either happens or it doesn't.

## Sample spaces: lists, tables and tree diagrams

With two or more things happening, list the outcomes **systematically**.

**List:** tossing a coin and spinning a spinner (red, blue): H-red, H-blue, T-red, T-blue.

**Table:** best for two dice. Put one die down the side, the other across the top, and fill in every cell. There are $6 \times 6 = 36$ equally likely outcomes. For the sum:

| + | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| **2** | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| **3** | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| **4** | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 |
| **5** | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 |
| **6** | 7 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |

A sum of 7 appears 6 times, so $P(\text{sum} = 7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Tree diagram:** best for two or three stages. Each stage branches out; each path from left to right is one outcome. Three coin tosses give $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$ outcomes: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT. So $P(\text{exactly two heads}) = \frac{3}{8}$ (HHT, HTH, THH).

## Two-way tables

A **two-way table** sorts people by two categories at once.

| | Pet | No pet | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Year 9** | 18 | 12 | 30 |
| **Year 10** | 14 | 16 | 30 |
| **Total** | 32 | 28 | 60 |

If one of these 60 students is chosen at random:

- $P(\text{has a pet}) = \frac{32}{60} = \frac{8}{15}$ (use the column total)
- $P(\text{Year 9 and has a pet}) = \frac{18}{60} = \frac{3}{10}$ (use one cell).

If we choose only from the **Year 9** students, there are 30 to choose from, so

$P(\text{has a pet, choosing from Year 9s}) = \frac{18}{30} = \frac{3}{5}$.

> **Watch out:** Read the question carefully to see who you're choosing from: everyone (divide by 60), or just one row or column (divide by that total).

## "Or": mutually exclusive events and double counting

Two events are **mutually exclusive** if they can't both happen at once. Rolling a 2 and rolling a 5 are mutually exclusive. For these, you can add:

$$P(2 \text{ or } 5) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{6}$$

If the events **can** happen together, adding counts some outcomes twice. Rolling an even number {2, 4, 6} and rolling a number greater than 3 {4, 5, 6} overlap on 4 and 6.

- Wrong: $\frac{3}{6} + \frac{3}{6} = 1$. That would mean it's certain, which is clearly false (you could roll a 1).
- Right: list the outcomes once each, {2, 4, 5, 6}, so $P = \frac{4}{6} = \frac{2}{3}$.

The general rule is $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$: you subtract the overlap so it is only counted once.

> **Tip:** In maths, "A or B" includes outcomes where both happen.

## Independent and dependent events

**Joint events** are two (or more) things happening one after the other.

- **Independent** events don't affect each other. Tossing a coin, then rolling a die: the coin can't change the die.
- **Dependent** events do affect each other. Taking a counter from a bag and **not putting it back** changes what's left for the second draw.

On a tree diagram with probabilities on the branches, **multiply along the branches** to get the probability of a path, then **add** the paths you want.

### Example

A bag has 3 red and 2 blue counters. Two are taken, **without** putting the first back.

- $P(\text{red, red}) = \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{2}{4} = \frac{6}{20}$: after a red is taken, 2 of the 4 left are red.
- If the first counter **is** put back, the draws are independent: $P(\text{red, red}) = \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{9}{25}$.

> **Watch out:** A fair coin has no memory. After five heads in a row, the next toss is still $\frac{1}{2}$ heads.

## Exam tips

- **Write the sample space** (list, table or tree) before counting. Be systematic so you don't miss outcomes.
- **Check your total:** a coin and a die give $2 \times 6 = 12$ outcomes; two dice give 36.
- **"Or"**: if the events overlap, count shared outcomes only once.
- **"Not"**: $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$ is often the quickest route.
- **Two-way tables:** decide who you're choosing from before you divide.
- **Simplify** fractions when the question asks for simplest form. A probability can never be more than 1, so check your answer.
