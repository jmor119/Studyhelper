Money, measurements and calculator answers all use decimals. This lesson covers calculating with decimals without a calculator, rounding sensibly, and using estimates to catch mistakes before they cost you marks.

## Adding and subtracting decimals

Line up the decimal points, one under the other. Fill any gaps with zeros so both numbers have the same number of **decimal places**, then add or subtract as normal.

### Example

$12 - 3.46$: write 12 as 12.00 so both numbers have 2 decimal places and the columns match up.

$12.00 - 3.46 = 8.54$

> **Watch out:** Don't line up the last digits. In $4.7 + 2.85$, the 7 is tenths and the 5 is hundredths, so they belong in different columns: $4.70 + 2.85 = 7.55$.

## Multiplying decimals

1. Multiply the numbers as if there were no decimal points.
2. Count the decimal places in the question altogether.
3. Give the answer that many decimal places.

### Example

$0.3 \times 0.04$: first $3 \times 4 = 12$. There are $1 + 2 = 3$ decimal places in the question, so the answer is $0.012$.

**Why it works:** as fractions, $0.3 \times 0.04 = \frac{3}{10} \times \frac{4}{100} = \frac{12}{1000} = 0.012$. Tenths times hundredths gives thousandths.

> **Tip:** Multiplying by a decimal between 0 and 1 makes a number *smaller*: $24 \times 0.5 = 12$ (half of 24).

## Dividing, and what to do with remainders

**Dividing by a decimal:** multiply both numbers by 10, 100, … until you are dividing by a whole number. The answer doesn't change.

$4.5 \div 0.09 = 450 \div 9 = 50$

A **remainder** can be written in different ways. For $17 \div 5$:

- remainder form: 3 remainder 2
- as a fraction: $3\frac{2}{5}$ (the remainder 2 out of 5)
- as a decimal: $3.4$

In a word problem, the context decides what to do with a remainder:

- 213 students and buses that seat 50: $213 \div 50 = 4.26$, so you need **5** buses. Round **up**, or 13 students are left behind.
- 250 eggs in cartons of 12: $250 \div 12 = 20.83\ldots$, so you can fill **20** cartons. Round **down**, because the last carton isn't full.

## Rounding to the precision you need

**Rounding** gives a simpler value that is close to the exact one. To round to a number of decimal places:

1. Find the last digit you want to keep.
2. Look at the next digit. If it is 5 or more, round up. If it is 4 or less, keep the digit as it is.
3. Drop the digits after it.

$6.4372$ to 2 decimal places: keep $6.43$. The next digit is 7, so round up to $6.44$.

$3.996$ to 2 decimal places is $4.00$. Keep the zeros: they show the answer is accurate to 2 decimal places.

**Precision** means how exact a value is. Choose a precision that suits the context:

- money: to the nearest cent (2 decimal places), e.g. \$17.43
- a person's height: to the nearest centimetre, not the nearest micrometre
- a number of people or buses: a whole number

> **Watch out:** Round only at the end. Rounding in the middle of a calculation can make your final answer wrong.

## Estimating to predict and check

An **estimate** is a quick, approximate answer. Round each number to something easy, such as the nearest whole number or nearest 10, then calculate.

### Example

Estimate $48.7 \times 3.1$. Round to $50 \times 3 = 150$. The exact answer is $150.97$, which is close.

Use estimates to check calculator answers. If you type $6.21 \times 38.9$ and get $2415.69$, estimate $6 \times 40 = 240$. The calculator answer is about 10 times too big, so a decimal point was probably typed in the wrong place. (The correct answer is $241.569$.)

## Exam tips

- **Line up the decimal points** for + and −. Count decimal places for ×.
- **Make the divisor a whole number** before dividing by a decimal.
- **Read what the question wants:** "to 2 decimal places", "to the nearest cent", "how many full boxes".
- **Keep trailing zeros when rounding:** 4.00 to 2 decimal places, not 4.
- **Estimate first.** A 5-second estimate catches most decimal-point mistakes.
