Drawing a graph is only half the job. The other half is saying what it shows, with numbers as evidence. In this lesson you'll learn how to describe a display, answer a statistical question, and spot a graph that could trick you.

## Describing a display

When you describe a graph, talk about:

- the **variable** and its units, and who or what the data is about
- the **shape**: how many peaks, and whether it is symmetrical or skewed
- the **centre**: where the middle of the data is (the median)
- the **spread**: from the minimum to the maximum, and the range
- anything else: the most common values, outliers, or a trend

## Answering questions with evidence

To answer a statistical question, use a measure of **centre** (usually the median) and a measure of **spread** (the range) as your evidence.

### Example

A class grew tomato seedlings in the sun and in the shade, then measured them.

| | Median height | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Sun | 15 cm | 7 cm |
| Shade | 12 cm | 9 cm |

"Seedlings in the sun **tend to** be taller. Their median height is 15 cm, compared with 12 cm in the shade. The shade heights are a little more spread out (a range of 9 cm, compared with 7 cm)."

Say "tend to", because the groups usually overlap. Some shade seedlings may still be taller than some sun seedlings.

> **Tip:** An outlier can make the range much bigger. Say so, for example: "The range is 30 minutes, but without the outlier it is only 8 minutes."

## Trends in time-series graphs

A **trend** is the overall direction of a time-series graph. It can be upward, downward, or there may be no clear trend. Look at the whole graph, not just one step.

## Spotting misleading graphs
