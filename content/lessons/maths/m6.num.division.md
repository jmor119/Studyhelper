Division helps you share things fairly and put things into equal groups: players into teams, students into vans, biscuits into bags. This lesson shows short division, how to write a remainder, and how to decide what to do with a remainder in a real problem.

## Sharing and grouping

To **divide** means to **share** into equal parts, or to make equal **groups**.

In $23 \div 5$:
- 5 is the **divisor**: the number you divide by.
- The answer is the **quotient**.
- Anything left over is the **remainder**.

Five fits into 23 four times ($4 \times 5 = 20$), with 3 left over. So $23 \div 5 = 4$ r 3. The "r" is short for remainder.

Times tables help, because dividing undoes multiplying. $7 \times 8 = 56$, so $56 \div 7 = 8$.

> **Watch out:** The remainder must be smaller than the divisor. If it isn't, another group fits.

## Short division

### Example: $1{,}283 \div 5$

1. 5 doesn't go into 1. Carry the 1 to make 12.
2. $12 \div 5 = 2$ r 2. Write 2 and carry the 2 to make 28.
3. $28 \div 5 = 5$ r 3. Write 5 and carry the 3 to make 33.
4. $33 \div 5 = 6$ r 3. Write 6. The 3 left over is the remainder.

So $1{,}283 \div 5 = 256$ r 3.

**Zeros matter.** In $4{,}218 \div 6$: $42 \div 6 = 7$. Then 6 doesn't go into 1, so write **0** and carry the 1 to make 18. $18 \div 6 = 3$. The answer is 703, not 73.

## Remainders as fractions and decimals

A remainder can also be written as a fraction or a decimal.

**As a fraction:** $31 \div 6 = 5$ r 1. The 1 left over is shared into 6 parts too, which gives $\frac{1}{6}$. So $31 \div 6 = 5\frac{1}{6}$.

This links to **unit fractions** (fractions with 1 on top). Finding $\frac{1}{6}$ of 31 means sharing 31 into 6 equal parts:

$\frac{1}{6}$ of $31 = 31 \div 6 = 5\frac{1}{6}$

**As a decimal:** $47 \div 4 = 11$ r 3. The remainder is $\frac{3}{4}$, and $\frac{3}{4} = 0.75$. So $47 \div 4 = 11.75$.

**With money:** 4 friends share \$50. $50 \div 4 = 12$ r 2, so each gets \$12 and there is \$2 left. \$2 is 200 cents, and $200 \div 4 = 50$ cents. Each friend gets **\$12.50**.

## What do I do with the remainder?

In a word problem, read the question carefully. The remainder can change the answer.

| If the question… | then… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| needs room for everyone | **round up** | 130 students, 8 in each van: $130 \div 8 = 16$ r 2, so you need **17 vans** |
| asks for full groups only | **round down** | 75 muffins in boxes of 6: $75 \div 6 = 12$ r 3, so **12 full boxes** |
| asks how many are left | use the **remainder** | 3 muffins are left over |
| shares something you can cut | use a **fraction** or **decimal** | 9 pizzas for 4 people: $2\frac{1}{4}$ pizzas each |

> **Tip:** Ask yourself, "Does the leftover part still need something, or is it just left out?"

## Check your answer

Multiplying undoes dividing, so you can always check a division.

**quotient × divisor + remainder = the number you started with**

For $1{,}283 \div 5 = 256$ r 3: $256 \times 5 = 1{,}280$, and $1{,}280 + 3 = 1{,}283$ ✓

This also helps you find a missing number. If $\square \div 7 = 128$ r 3, then $\square = 128 \times 7 + 3 = 896 + 3 = 899$.

**Estimate too.** $1{,}283 \div 5$ is close to $1{,}250 \div 5 = 250$, so 256 is a sensible answer.

> **Exam tips:**
> - Remember to write a 0 in the answer when the divisor doesn't go into a digit.
> - The remainder must be smaller than the divisor.
> - In word problems, finish with a sentence that answers the question.
