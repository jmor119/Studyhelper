Cereal boxes, triangular chocolate boxes, tents and the pyramids of Egypt are all 3D shapes. In this lesson you'll name prisms and pyramids, count their faces, edges and vertices, unfold them into nets, and draw them from above, the front and the side.

## Prisms and pyramids

A **prism** is a 3D shape with two identical, parallel ends joined by rectangles. If you slice it parallel to an end, every slice (**cross-section**) is the same shape as the ends. A prism is named after the shape of its ends: triangular prism, hexagonal prism, and so on. A cuboid is a rectangular prism, and a cube is a special cuboid.

A **pyramid** has one flat **base** and triangular faces that meet at a point called the **apex**. It is named after its base: square-based pyramid, pentagonal pyramid, and so on. A triangular pyramid is also called a **tetrahedron**.

> **Watch out:** Cylinders, cones and spheres have curved surfaces, so they are not prisms or pyramids.

## Faces, edges and vertices

- A **face** is a flat surface.
- An **edge** is a line where two faces meet.
- A **vertex** is a corner where edges meet. (Plural: vertices.)

If the end of a prism, or the base of a pyramid, has $n$ sides:

| | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prism | $n + 2$ | $3n$ | $2n$ |
| Pyramid | $n + 1$ | $2n$ | $n + 1$ |

For example, a hexagonal prism ($n = 6$) has 8 faces, 18 edges and 12 vertices.

> **Tip:** Check with Euler's rule: for these shapes, faces + vertices − edges = 2. For the hexagonal prism, $8 + 12 - 18 = 2$ ✓.

## Nets

A **net** is a flat pattern that folds up to make a 3D shape. Each shape in the net becomes one face.

- A prism's net usually has a strip of rectangles (the sides) with the two end shapes attached.
- A pyramid's net has the base in the middle with a triangle on each edge. The triangles fold up to meet at the apex.
- A cube has **11** different nets, all made of 6 squares. Not every arrangement of 6 squares works. If two squares would fold onto the same face, one face is left open.

**Opposite faces of a cube.** In a straight line of three squares in a cube net, the first and third squares end up **opposite** each other. Squares that share an edge in the net always end up **next to** each other.

## Plan, front and side views

A 3D object can be drawn as flat views:

- **Plan view**: looking straight down from above.
- **Front view** (front elevation): looking straight at the front.
- **Side view** (side elevation): looking straight at one side, usually from the right.

Each view is drawn as a flat 2D shape, with no perspective.

For a solid made of stacked cubes, a plan with numbers on it shows how many cubes are in each stack. Then:

- the **plan view** has one square for every stack;
- in the **front view**, each column is as tall as the **tallest** stack in that column of the plan (shorter stacks behind are hidden);
- the **side view** works the same way, using the rows of the plan.

### Example

A plan has a back row of 2, 0, 1 and a front row of 1, 1, 3. The front view has columns 2, 1, 3 squares tall (the tallest in each column), so it has 6 squares. The solid uses $2 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 3 = 8$ cubes.

## Exam tips

- Name prisms by their ends and pyramids by their base.
- Learn the face, edge and vertex rules, and check with $F + V - E = 2$.
- When counting edges or vertices from a net, remember that edges and corners join up when it is folded. Work out the solid first, then count.
- For views, imagine standing exactly where the arrow says, and remember that tall stacks hide shorter ones behind them.
