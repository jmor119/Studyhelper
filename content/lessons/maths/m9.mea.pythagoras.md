Builders, surveyors and game designers all use one famous fact about right-angled triangles. If you know the two shorter sides, you can work out the longest side without measuring it. You can also check whether a corner really is a right angle. This lesson covers **Pythagoras' theorem**: what it says, how to use it, and why it's true.

## The hypotenuse

In a right-angled triangle, the side **opposite the right angle** is called the **hypotenuse**. It is always the **longest** side.

To find it, look for the small square that marks the **right angle**. The hypotenuse is the one side that does **not** touch that square. The other two sides (the shorter ones) meet at the right angle.

> **Tip:** Before doing any calculation, label the hypotenuse $c$ and the shorter sides $a$ and $b$.

## Pythagoras' theorem

For any right-angled triangle with shorter sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$:

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$

In words: **the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides.** If you draw a square on each side of the triangle, the two smaller squares have the same total area as the big one.

### Example

The shorter sides are 6 cm and 8 cm.

$c^2 = 6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100$, so $c = \sqrt{100} = 10$ cm.

## Finding the hypotenuse

1. Check the triangle is right-angled and find the hypotenuse.
2. Square the two shorter sides and **add** them.
3. Take the **square root** to get $c$.
4. Round sensibly and include units.

### Example

A ladder reaches 4.5 m up a wall, with its foot 1.2 m from the wall. How long is the ladder?

The wall and ground meet at a right angle, so the ladder is the hypotenuse.

$c^2 = 4.5^2 + 1.2^2 = 20.25 + 1.44 = 21.69$

$c = \sqrt{21.69} = 4.657\ldots \approx 4.7$ m

> **Watch out:** Square first, add second. $4.5 + 1.2 = 5.7$, then $5.7^2$, is **not** the same thing.

## Checking for a right angle, and Pythagorean triples

The theorem also works backwards. If the sides of a triangle fit $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ (with $c$ the longest side), then the triangle **is** right-angled. If they don't fit, it isn't.

- 7, 24, 25: $49 + 576 = 625 = 25^2$ ✓ right-angled.
- 6, 9, 11: $36 + 81 = 117$, but $11^2 = 121$ ✗ not right-angled.

A **Pythagorean triple** is a set of three whole numbers that fit the theorem. Useful ones to know: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25).

Multiplying every number in a triple by the same amount gives another triple: (3, 4, 5) × 2 = (6, 8, 10), and × 10 gives (30, 40, 50). Builders use this to set out a square corner: measure 3 m along one side and 4 m along the other. If the distance between those two points is exactly 5 m, the corner is a right angle.

## Why it works

Here is one way to prove the theorem. Take four identical right-angled triangles with shorter sides $a$ and $b$ and hypotenuse $c$.

**Picture 1.** Arrange them inside a big square with sides $a + b$, one triangle in each corner, with their hypotenuses facing inwards. The space left in the middle is a square with sides $c$, so it has area $c^2$.

**Picture 2.** Slide the same four triangles around inside the same big square so they pair up into two rectangles. Now the space left over is two squares: one with area $a^2$ and one with area $b^2$.

The big square and the four triangles are the same in both pictures, so the leftover space must be the same too:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$

## Exam tips

- Find the right angle first. The side opposite it is $c$.
- Square, add, then square root. Don't add the sides first.
- Keep full calculator values until the end, then round as asked.
- Checking for a right angle? Compare $a^2 + b^2$ with $c^2$ for the **longest** side, and state your conclusion in words.
- Recognise triples and their multiples to save time, especially on non-calculator papers.
