Statistics is about learning from data. A good investigation starts with a clear question, then plans exactly what data to collect and how. Get the question and the plan right and everything after them gets easier.

## Statistical questions

A **statistical question** is one you answer using data that **varies**. "How tall is Mere?" isn't statistical, because one measurement answers it. "What is the typical height of Year 9 students at our school?" is statistical, because heights vary from student to student.

A good statistical question names three things:

| Part | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Variable** | what you measure or record | arm span (cm) |
| **Group** (population) | who or what the data is about | Year 9 students at our school |
| **Intent** | what you want to find out | what is typical, a comparison, or a relationship |

So "What is the typical arm span of Year 9 students at our school?" names the variable (arm span), the group (Year 9 students at our school) and the intent (find what is typical).

> **Watch out:** "Are Year 9s good at sport?" is too vague. What would you measure? A good question uses a variable you can actually record.

## Types of variable

A **variable** is any characteristic that can change from one person or thing to the next.

- A **categorical** variable puts each person or thing into a group: favourite sport, eye colour, way of getting to school.
- A **numerical** variable is a count or a measurement: number of siblings, height in cm, time in minutes.

Some numbers are really labels. A postcode or a rugby jersey number is **categorical**: you can't sensibly add two postcodes or find the mean jersey number.

**Multivariate** data records more than two variables for each person. A class survey might record each student's year level, gender, height, arm span and way of getting to school. When you plan your own investigation, collect at least one categorical and one numerical variable.

## Four types of investigation

The intent of your question decides the type of investigation.

| Type | What it looks at | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| **Summary** | one variable for one group | What is the typical arm span of Year 9 students at our school? |
| **Comparison** | one variable for two groups | Do Year 9 students who walk to school tend to have shorter travel times than those who are driven? |
| **Relationship** | two variables for the same people or things | Is there a relationship between height and arm span for Year 9 students? |
| **Time-series** | one variable over time | How has the number of students at our school changed each year since 2015? |

> **Tip:** Count the groups and the variables. One of each is a summary. Two groups is a comparison. Two variables paired up for each person is a relationship. Anything measured over time is a time-series.

A relationship investigation can use two numerical variables (height and arm span) or two categorical variables (year level and favourite sport).

## Collecting the data

**Primary data** is data you collect yourself, for example by measuring, surveying your class or running an experiment. **Secondary data** was collected by someone else, such as Stats NZ figures, a sports website or a data set from CensusAtSchool (an online survey that many NZ schools take part in).

In an **observational study** you measure or record things as they are, without changing anything. Recording how long each student takes to get to school is observational. If you deliberately changed something, such as giving one group extra practice before a test, that would be an experiment instead.

Planning checklist:

1. Write your statistical question.
2. Decide on your variables, with at least one categorical and one numerical.
3. Decide **how** to measure each one, including units (cm, minutes) and how accurately.
4. Decide who you will collect data from and how.
5. Make a recording sheet or table with one row per person.

> **Watch out:** Measure everyone the same way. If some arm spans are measured with shoes on a tape and others by guessing, your data won't be reliable.

## Exam tips

- **Statistical question:** check it has a variable, a group and an intent, and that the answer needs data that varies.
- **Variable type:** ask "is this a count or a measurement?" If yes, it's numerical. If it's a group or label, it's categorical. Numbers that are labels (postcodes, jersey numbers, phone numbers) are categorical.
- **Investigation type:** one group (summary), two groups (comparison), two variables (relationship) or over time (time-series).
- **Primary or secondary:** did *you* collect it? Then it's primary.
- When asked to plan, be specific: say exactly what you will measure, in what units, and how.
