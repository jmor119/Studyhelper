How much fencing goes around a paddock? How far is one lap of a running track? These are **perimeter** questions. Straight-sided shapes are easy: add the sides. Circles need a special number, π, and that's the main idea of this lesson.

## Perimeter of straight-sided shapes

The **perimeter** is the total distance around the outside of a shape. Add up the lengths of all the sides, making sure they are in the same unit.

- Rectangle: $P = 2 \times (\text{length} + \text{width})$
- Square: $P = 4 \times \text{side}$
- Regular polygon (all sides equal): $P = \text{number of sides} \times \text{side}$

Some shapes, such as L-shapes, have sides that aren't labelled. Work them out first. In an L-shape the two short horizontal edges add up to the long horizontal edge, and the same is true for the vertical edges.

### Example

An L-shape has a bottom edge of 12 m, a left edge of 9 m, a top edge of 5 m and a short right edge of 4 m. The missing horizontal edge is $12 - 5 = 7$ m and the missing vertical edge is $9 - 4 = 5$ m.

Perimeter $= 12 + 4 + 7 + 5 + 5 + 9 = 42$ m.

> **Tip:** Tick off each side as you add it so you don't miss one or count one twice.

## Circles and π

For a circle, the perimeter is called the **circumference**. Two other lengths matter:

- The **radius** ($r$) goes from the centre to the circle.
- The **diameter** ($d$) goes right across through the centre. $d = 2r$.

If you measure the circumference and diameter of any circle (a coin, a plate, a bike wheel) and divide, you always get the same number, a little more than 3. This number is called **pi**, written **π**:

$$\pi = \frac{C}{d} \approx 3.14159\ldots$$

Rearranging gives the circumference formulas:

$$C = \pi d \qquad \text{or} \qquad C = 2\pi r$$

### Example

Find the circumference of a circle with radius 6 cm.

$C = 2 \times \pi \times 6 = 37.699\ldots \approx 37.7$ cm (to 1 decimal place).

> **Watch out:** Check whether you've been given the radius or the diameter. Using the radius in $C = \pi d$ gives an answer that is half the size it should be.

## Working backwards

Because $\pi = C \div d$, you can find the diameter when you know the circumference:

$$d = C \div \pi$$

Then halve it if you need the radius.

### Example

A tree trunk has a circumference of 150 cm. How wide is it (what is its diameter)?

$d = 150 \div \pi = 47.746\ldots \approx 47.7$ cm.

So the tree is about 48 cm across. Arborists use this trick to work out a trunk's diameter with a tape measure.

## Half circles and quarter circles

A **semicircle** is half a circle. Its perimeter has **two** parts: the curved edge **and** the straight edge (a diameter).

$$P_{\text{semicircle}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi d + d$$

A **quarter circle** has a curved edge that is a quarter of the circumference, plus **two** straight edges, which are both radii:

$$P_{\text{quarter}} = \tfrac{1}{4} \times 2\pi r + 2r = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi r + 2r$$

### Example

Find the perimeter of a semicircle with diameter 10 m.

Curved edge: $\frac{1}{2} \times \pi \times 10 = 15.707\ldots$ m. Straight edge: 10 m.

Perimeter $= 15.707\ldots + 10 = 25.707\ldots \approx 25.7$ m.

> **Watch out:** The most common mistake is to find only the curved part. Always ask: "Which straight edges are on the outside of this shape?"

## Composite shapes

Many shapes are made of rectangles and parts of circles, such as a window with a semicircle on top or a running track with semicircular ends. To find the perimeter:

1. Sketch the shape and trace its **outside** edge with your finger.
2. List each part: straight edges and curved edges.
3. Lines **inside** the shape (where the pieces join) are **not** part of the perimeter.

A running track with two straights of 85 m and semicircle ends of diameter 70 m: the two semicircles make one full circle, so one lap is $2 \times 85 + \pi \times 70 = 170 + 219.9\ldots \approx 389.9$ m.

## Exam tips

- Use the π button and only round at the very end. Rounding halfway through can change your last digit.
- Radius or diameter? Read carefully, and use $d = 2r$ if you need to swap.
- Semicircle: half the circumference **plus** the diameter. Quarter circle: a quarter of the circumference **plus** two radii.
- Give units (cm, m) and the rounding asked for, such as "1 decimal place".
