A **number sentence** is a maths sentence made from numbers and signs, like $6 + 4 = 10$. In this lesson you will decide if number sentences are true or false, find missing numbers, and use the signs $<$, $>$, $\le$ and $\ge$.

## True or false?

A number sentence can be **true** or **false**.

- $5 + 3 = 8$ is true.
- $5 + 3 = 9$ is false.

The **equals** sign ($=$) means "has the same value as". The two sides must be worth the same amount, like a balanced scale. It does **not** mean "the answer comes next".

### Example

Is $4 \times 6 = 30 - 6$ true or false?

1. Work out the left side: $4 \times 6 = 24$.
2. Work out the right side: $30 - 6 = 24$.
3. Both sides are 24, so the number sentence is **true**.

> **Tip:** Work out each side on its own first. Then compare the two answers.

## Missing numbers

An **open number sentence** has a missing number. The gap is often shown as a box: $\square + 7 = 15$. Your job is to find the number that makes the sentence true.

Use the opposite operation to undo what has been done:

- $\square + 7 = 15$. Take away 7: $15 - 7 = 8$. So $\square = 8$.
- $\square \times 6 = 42$. Divide by 6: $42 \div 6 = 7$. So $\square = 7$.

Always check by putting your number back in: $8 + 7 = 15$ ✓

### Example

$9 + 6 = \square + 10$

1. The left side is $9 + 6 = 15$.
2. So the right side must be 15 too: $\square + 10 = 15$.
3. $15 - 10 = 5$, so $\square = 5$.

> **Watch out:** The answer is not 15. 15 is the value of each side. The box needs 5.

## Inequality signs

An **inequality** compares two amounts that may not be equal.

| Sign | Say it | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| $<$ | is **less than** | $3 < 8$ |
| $>$ | is **greater than** | $9 > 2$ |
| $\le$ | is **less than or equal to** | $3 \le 4$ and $4 \le 4$ |
| $\ge$ | is **greater than or equal to** | $7 \ge 5$ and $7 \ge 7$ |

The open side of the sign faces the bigger number. The point faces the smaller number.

$\le$ and $\ge$ have a line underneath. The line means "or equal to".

### Example

Is $6 \times 2 \ge 12$ true?

$6 \times 2 = 12$, and 12 is equal to 12. $\ge$ allows "equal to", so it is **true**.

But $6 \times 2 > 12$ is **false**, because 12 is not greater than 12.

## Using the order of operations

When a side has more than one operation, follow the **order of operations**:

1. Brackets first.
2. Then squares, like $4^2$.
3. Then $\times$ and $\div$, from left to right.
4. Then $+$ and $-$, from left to right.

### Example

Is $8 \times 7 \le 8 \times 5 + 4^2$ true or false?

- Left side: $8 \times 7 = 56$.
- Right side: $8 \times 5 + 4^2 = 8 \times 5 + 16 = 40 + 16 = 56$.
- $56 \le 56$ is **true**, because $\le$ includes "equal to".

> **Watch out:** $20 - 3 \times 4$ is $20 - 12 = 8$. Don't work from left to right ($17 \times 4$).

## Putting it together

- Work out each side on its own, then compare.
- $=$ means both sides have the same value.
- $<$ and $>$ do **not** include "equal to". $\le$ and $\ge$ do.
- To find a missing number, undo the operations. Then check by putting your number back in.
- Use the order of operations: brackets, squares, $\times$ and $\div$, then $+$ and $-$.

### Example

What is the largest whole number that makes $\square \times 4 < 30$ true?

Count in 4s: $7 \times 4 = 28$, which is less than 30 ✓. $8 \times 4 = 32$, which is too big ✗.

So the largest whole number is **7**.
