You can move a shape by flipping it, sliding it or turning it. These moves are called transformations. In this lesson you will do them on a grid, do two in a row, and make patterns that fit together with no gaps.

## Three ways to move a shape

A **transformation** moves a shape to a new place. The new shape is called the **image**.

- A **reflection** flips the shape over a **mirror line**. The image is a mirror image.
- A **translation** slides the shape. It does not turn or flip.
- A **rotation** turns the shape around a fixed point, called the **centre of rotation**.

## Reflections

In a reflection, every point of the shape moves straight across the mirror line. It lands the **same distance** from the line, on the other side.

### Example

Shape A is 1 square to the left of the mirror line, so its image B is 1 square to the right of the line. The top of the shape is still at the top, but B faces the other way, like your reflection in a mirror.

To reflect a shape on a grid:

1. Pick a corner of the shape.
2. Count how many squares it is from the mirror line.
3. Count the same number of squares on the other side, and mark a dot.
4. Do this for every corner, then join the dots.

> **Watch out:** Don't just slide the shape across the line. A reflection must face the opposite way.

## Translations and rotations

To describe a **translation**, say how many squares the shape moves left or right, and how many squares it moves up or down. Follow one corner and count the jumps.

### Example

The dot on shape A moves 4 squares right and 2 squares up to reach shape B. So the translation is 4 squares right and 2 squares up.

A **rotation** turns a shape. A **quarter turn** is 90°, a **half turn** is 180° and a full turn is 360°. **Clockwise** is the way the hands of a clock move. **Anticlockwise** is the other way.

To rotate a shape, imagine pinning it to the page at the centre of rotation, then turning it. Tracing paper really helps.

## Tessellations

A **tessellation** is a pattern of shapes that fit together with **no gaps and no overlaps**, like tiles on a floor or the cells in a honeycomb. You make one by translating, rotating or reflecting a shape again and again. Kōwhaiwhai patterns, painted on the rafters of a wharenui, also repeat a design by sliding and flipping it.

Where tiles meet at a point, their corners must add up to exactly 360° (a full turn).

### Example

Each corner of a regular hexagon is 120°. Three hexagons fit around a point because $3 \times 120° = 360°$. Each corner of a regular pentagon is 108°. Three pentagons make $3 \times 108° = 324°$, which leaves a 36° gap, and four would overlap. So regular pentagons do not tessellate on their own.

Squares, equilateral triangles and regular hexagons all tessellate on their own.
