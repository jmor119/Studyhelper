A graph turns an equation into a picture. Equations like $y = 2x + 1$ always make a straight line, and you can read a lot from the line straight away: where it crosses the axes, and whether it goes up or down.

## Coordinates and quadrants

A **coordinate** pair $(x, y)$ gives a point's position: go across $x$ first, then up or down $y$. The point $(0, 0)$, where the axes cross, is the **origin**.

The axes split the grid into four **quadrants**:

| Quadrant | $x$ | $y$ | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| First (top right) | positive | positive | $(3, 2)$ |
| Second (top left) | negative | positive | $(-3, 2)$ |
| Third (bottom left) | negative | negative | $(-3, -2)$ |
| Fourth (bottom right) | positive | negative | $(3, -2)$ |

> **Tip:** "Along the corridor, then up the stairs": $x$ first, then $y$.

> **Watch out:** $(2, 5)$ and $(5, 2)$ are different points.

## Tables of values and plotting

To draw $y = 2x - 1$:

1. Choose some $x$ values, for example $-2$ to $2$.
2. Substitute each one to find $y$.
3. Plot the points and join them with a ruler. Extend the line to the edge of the grid.

| $x$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $y$ | $-5$ | $-3$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $3$ |

For $x = -2$: $y = 2 \times (-2) - 1 = -5$.

Every point on the line makes the equation true, and every solution of the equation is a point on the line. Between and beyond the plotted points there are infinitely many more, such as $(0.5, 0)$. A line is the **infinite set of solutions** of its equation.

Because the line is straight, the $y$ values in the table change by the same amount each time: here, up 2 for each step of 1 in $x$. That's why these graphs are called **linear**.

> **Tip:** If one plotted point isn't in line with the others, recheck that calculation.

## The y-intercept

The **$y$-intercept** is where the line crosses the $y$-axis. At that point $x = 0$.

For $y = mx + c$, put $x = 0$: $y = m \times 0 + c = c$. So the line crosses the $y$-axis at $(0, c)$.

| Equation | $y$-intercept |
|---|---|
| $y = 2x + 5$ | $(0, 5)$ |
| $y = 3x - 4$ | $(0, -4)$ |
| $y = 7 - x$ | $(0, 7)$ |
| $y = 4x$ | $(0, 0)$, the origin |

You can find $c$ three ways:

- **From the equation:** it's the number term.
- **From a graph:** read where the line crosses the $y$-axis.
- **From a table:** it's the $y$ value when $x = 0$. If $x = 0$ isn't in the table, work backwards using the constant difference.

> **Watch out:** in $y = 7 - x$ the terms are in a different order. The $y$-intercept is still the number term, 7.

## Gradient: which way, and how steep

The **gradient**, or **slope**, describes how steep a line is and which way it goes. In $y = mx + c$, the gradient is $m$, the number in front of $x$.

Read the line from left to right:

- **Positive gradient** ($m > 0$): the line goes **up**.
- **Negative gradient** ($m < 0$): the line goes **down**.
- **Zero gradient** ($m = 0$): the line is **horizontal**. Its equation is $y = c$, such as $y = 3$.

The bigger the size of $m$ (ignoring its sign), the steeper the line. $y = 5x$ is steeper than $y = 2x$, and $y = -4x$ is steeper than $y = x$.

> **Watch out:** a horizontal line is $y = $ a number, because every point has the same $y$-coordinate. A **vertical** line is $x = $ a number, for example $x = 2$.

From a table, look at the $y$ values as $x$ increases: if they go up, $m$ is positive; if they go down, $m$ is negative; if they stay the same, $m = 0$.

## Putting it together

For any line $y = mx + c$:

| Feature | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| $c$ | where the line crosses the $y$-axis, at $(0, c)$ |
| sign of $m$ | up (positive), down (negative) or flat (zero) |
| size of $m$ | how steep the line is |

**Exam tips**

- **Use a ruler** and extend the line across the whole grid. Label it with its equation.
- **Plot at least three points.** Two points make a line, and the third is your check.
- **Brackets for negative $x$ values** when you substitute, e.g. $2 \times (-3)$.
- **To test a point,** substitute its $x$ value. If you get its $y$ value, the point is on the line.
- **Horizontal means $y = c$**, with gradient 0.
