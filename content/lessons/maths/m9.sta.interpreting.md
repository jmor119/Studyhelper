Calculating statistics is only half the job. You also need to say what they mean, in context, without claiming more than the data can show. And you need to be able to spot when someone else's graph or claim is misleading.

## Describing what the data shows

A good description of a distribution talks about:

- **centre**: what's typical (usually the median)
- **spread**: how much the values vary (IQR or range)
- **shape**: where the values bunch up and whether one side stretches out further
- **unusual values**: any **outliers**, values far away from the rest.

Always write in **context**, with numbers and units as **evidence**.

### Example

Weak: "The median is 18."

Better: "The median travel time for these Year 9 students was 18 minutes. The middle half of the students took between 12 and 25 minutes (IQR = 13 minutes). One student took 70 minutes, much longer than everyone else."

> **Tip:** A strong statement has three parts: the **claim**, the **evidence** (a statistic with its unit) and the **context** (what the numbers are about).

## Variation, chance and uncertainty

Data always varies. If you measured another class, you would get slightly different results just by chance. So claims need to allow for **uncertainty**:

| Overclaiming | Careful |
|---|---|
| "Year 13s are taller than Year 9s." | "In our data, Year 13 students **tended to be** taller than Year 9 students." |
| "This proves screen time causes less sleep." | "Students with more screen time **tended to** sleep less in this data." |
| "Every Room 4 student scored higher." | "Room 4 had a higher median score, but the scores **overlap**." |

Words like *tend to*, *about*, *in this data* and *for these students* show you understand that your data is just one set of results.

> **Watch out:** A relationship in a scatter plot doesn't prove that one thing **causes** the other. Students who sleep more might also do something else that matters.

## Outliers

An **outlier** is a value that is much larger or much smaller than the rest of the data.

When you find one, ask: is it a **mistake** (e.g. 1.62 typed as 162), or a **genuine** unusual value (e.g. a student who lives a long way from school)? Don't just delete it. Mistakes should be corrected or removed, but genuine values are part of the data.

Outliers affect statistics differently:

- The **mean** is pulled towards an outlier, sometimes a lot.
- The **median** and **IQR** hardly change.

### Example

Times (minutes): 8, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 70.

- With the outlier: mean $= 140 \div 7 = 20$ minutes, median = 12 minutes.
- Without it: mean $= 70 \div 6 \approx 11.7$ minutes, median = 11.5 minutes.

The mean dropped by more than 8 minutes, but the median moved only half a minute.

## Spotting misleading graphs

Graphs in the media, in adverts and on social media can be **misleading**, sometimes by accident and sometimes on purpose. Check for:

- **An axis that doesn't start at 0.** Small differences look huge. A bar for 42 can look three times as tall as a bar for 40.
- **Uneven scales**: numbers on an axis that aren't evenly spaced.
- **Pictures instead of bars**: if a picture is made twice as tall *and* twice as wide, its area is four times as big.
- **A cherry-picked time range**: only showing the months that support the claim.
- **Missing information**: no title, no axis labels, no units, or no idea how many people were asked.

> **Tip:** Always read the numbers on the scale, not just the heights of the bars.

## Evaluating how the data was collected

Even a perfect graph can be misleading if the data is poor. Ask:

- **Who was asked?** A survey about exercise done only at a gym isn't **representative** of all students. This is called **bias**.
- **How many?** Results from 5 people vary much more than results from 500.
- **How was the question worded?** A **leading question** such as "Don't you agree the canteen is too expensive?" pushes people towards one answer.
- **Who collected it, and why?** A company's survey about its own product might not be neutral.
- **Self-reported data** (people saying how much they sleep or exercise) may not be accurate.

## Exam tips

- Write **claim + evidence + context**: "The median travel time was 18 minutes for walkers and 9 minutes for bus users, so the walkers tended to take longer."
- Use careful words: *tend to*, *about*, *in this data*. Avoid *always*, *all*, *proves*.
- For outliers, say whether you think it's a mistake or genuine, and why.
- When critiquing a graph, name the problem **and** explain its effect ("the axis starts at 40, so the difference looks bigger than it is").
- When evaluating data collection, think: who, how many, how asked, and who by.
