A list of numbers is hard to make sense of. Summary statistics boil it down to a few numbers: where the middle is, and how spread out the values are. These are the numbers you'll quote as evidence in any statistics answer.

## Mean, median and mode

These three statistics each describe a "typical" value.

- **Mean**: add all the values, then divide by how many there are.
- **Median**: the middle value once the data is **in order**. With an even number of values, it is halfway between the two middle values.
- **Mode**: the value that occurs most often. There can be more than one mode, or none.

### Example

Kai's netball team scored 24, 31, 18, 31, 26 and 20 goals.

- Mean: $24 + 31 + 18 + 31 + 26 + 20 = 150$, and $150 \div 6 = 25$ goals.
- Median: in order, 18, 20, **24, 26**, 31, 31. The two middle values are 24 and 26, so the median is $\dfrac{24 + 26}{2} = 25$ goals.
- Mode: 31 goals (it appears twice).

> **Watch out:** Always put the data in order before finding the median. The middle of the *unordered* list is a very common wrong answer.

## Range: a first look at spread

Two classes can have the same mean but very different data. Measures of **spread** tell you how far apart the values are.

The **range** is the simplest:

$$\text{range} = \text{maximum} - \text{minimum}$$

For Kai's team, the range is $31 - 18 = 13$ goals.

The range only uses the two most extreme values. One unusual value (an **outlier**) can make it much bigger, so on its own it can give a misleading picture of spread.

> **Tip:** Always give units with summary statistics: "the median is 25 goals", "the range is 13 minutes".

## Quartiles and the five-number summary

The median splits the ordered data into a lower half and an upper half. The **quartiles** split it further, into quarters:

- the **lower quartile** ($Q_1$) is the median of the lower half
- the **upper quartile** ($Q_3$) is the median of the upper half.

**The method we use (the NZ curriculum method):** put the data in order and find the median. The median splits the data into two halves. **If there is an odd number of values, leave the median out of both halves.** Then $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ are the medians of the two halves.

### Example (odd number of values)

3, 5, 6, 8, **9**, 11, 12, 15, 20: there are 9 values, so the median is the 5th value, 9.

- Lower half (without the 9): 3, 5, 6, 8, so $Q_1 = \dfrac{5 + 6}{2} = 5.5$
- Upper half: 11, 12, 15, 20, so $Q_3 = \dfrac{12 + 15}{2} = 13.5$

The **five-number summary** is minimum, $Q_1$, median, $Q_3$, maximum: here 3, 5.5, 9, 13.5, 20.

### Example (even number of values)

4, 7, 8, 10 | 12, 15, 18, 21: the median is $\dfrac{10 + 12}{2} = 11$. The halves are 4, 7, 8, 10 and 12, 15, 18, 21, so $Q_1 = 7.5$ and $Q_3 = 16.5$.

> **Watch out:** Calculators and spreadsheets don't all use the same quartile method, so they can give slightly different answers. Use the method above.

## The interquartile range

The **interquartile range** (IQR) is the spread of the middle half of the data:

$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$$

For 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 15, 20, the IQR is $13.5 - 5.5 = 8$.

About half of the values lie between $Q_1$ and $Q_3$. Because the IQR ignores the top and bottom quarters, an outlier hardly affects it. That makes it a more reliable measure of spread than the range when the data has extreme values.

### Using a frequency table

When data is in a frequency table, the values are already sorted. Add the frequencies to find $n$, work out the position of the median, then count along using running totals.

| Number of pets | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 4 | 7 | 6 | 3 |

There are $4 + 7 + 6 + 3 = 20$ values, so the median is halfway between the 10th and 11th. The 0s are values 1–4 and the 1s are values 5–11, so both the 10th and 11th values are 1, and the median is 1 pet. The mean is $\dfrac{0 \times 4 + 1 \times 7 + 2 \times 6 + 3 \times 3}{20} = \dfrac{28}{20} = 1.4$ pets.

## Exam tips

- **Order the data first** for the median, quartiles and range. Cross values off as you go so you don't miss or repeat any.
- **Count the values** before finding the median. Odd $n$: one middle value. Even $n$: halfway between two.
- **Quartiles:** median of each half, leaving the median out when $n$ is odd.
- **Show working:** write the ordered list, the halves, and the calculation (e.g. $\text{IQR} = 31 - 18 = 13$). Method marks matter.
- **Missing value questions:** total = mean × number of values.
- **Check your answer makes sense:** a mean or median must lie between the smallest and largest values.
