When you collect data, it helps to sum it up with one or two numbers. An **average** tells you about a typical value. The **range** tells you how spread out the data is. In this lesson you'll learn four ways to describe data, and how to choose the best one.

## Types of data

A **variable** is something you count, measure or ask about, like height or favourite sport. There are three kinds.

- **Categorical** variables put things into groups: favourite fruit, eye colour, how you get to school.
- **Discrete numerical** variables are **counted**: the number of siblings, the goals in a game. The values are whole numbers.
- **Continuous numerical** variables are **measured**: height, mass, time. They can be any value, like 1.47 m or 12.3 seconds.

### Example

- "How many pets do you have?" The answer is counted, so it is discrete numerical.
- "How long is your arm span?" The answer is measured, so it is continuous numerical.
- "What is your favourite colour?" The answer is a group, so it is categorical.

> **Tip:** Ask yourself: is the answer a word, a count or a measurement?

## Mode and range

The **mode** is the value that appears most often. It is the only average you can use for categorical data.

The **range** is the highest value take away the lowest value. It measures **spread**: how far apart the data is.

### Example

A netball team scored these goals in 7 games: 12, 15, 9, 15, 11, 15, 10

- **Mode:** 15 appears three times, more than any other value. The mode is 15.
- **Range:** the highest value is 15 and the lowest is 9. The range is $15 - 9 = 6$.

> **Watch out:** The range is one number (6), not "9 to 15".

Some data sets have two modes. If every value appears only once, there is no mode.

## The median

The **median** is the middle value when the data is in order.

1. Write the values in order, from smallest to largest.
2. Cross off one value from each end until you reach the middle.
3. If there are two middle values, the median is halfway between them. Add them and divide by 2.

$$\text{mean} = \text{total of the values} \div \text{number of values}$$

### Example

Hemi scored 7, 9, 6, 8 and 10 in five quizzes.

- Total: $7 + 9 + 6 + 8 + 10 = 40$
- There are 5 quizzes, so the mean is $40 \div 5 = 8$.

The mean, median and mode are all measures of **central tendency**: each one describes the centre of the data.

**Working backwards:** if 4 values have a mean of 6, their total must be $4 \times 6 = 24$. This helps you find a missing value.

## Outliers and choosing an average

An **outlier** is a value that is much bigger or much smaller than the rest. Outliers aren't always mistakes, because rare things do happen.

### Example

Ages at a birthday party: 11, 12, 11, 12, 11, 75 (Koro came too).

- Mean: $132 \div 6 = 22$. That is older than nearly everyone at the party!
- Median: in order, 11, 11, **11**, **12**, 12, 75, so the median is $(11 + 12) \div 2 = 11.5$.

The outlier **pulls the mean towards it**, but it hardly changes the median. It also makes the range huge ($75 - 11 = 64$), so say so when you use the range.

| Use the… | when… |
|---|---|
| mode | the data is categorical, or you want the most common value |
| median | there is an outlier |
| mean | there are no outliers and you want to use every value |
