Graphs turn a list of numbers into a picture, so you can see patterns quickly. In this lesson you will read bar graphs, dot plots and time-series graphs, spot trends, and check graphs you see in the news.

## What makes a good graph

Every good graph has:

- a **title** that says what the graph is about
- a label on each **axis**, with units if there are any (like cm or °C)
- a **scale** that starts at 0 and goes up in equal steps

## Bar graphs and dot plots

A **bar graph** suits data sorted into groups (categories), like favourite sports or ways of getting to school.

## Time-series data

**Time-series data** has two numbers for each point, and one of them is a time. For example, you could weigh a bag of carrots every day for 5 days, or measure a sunflower every week.

We show time-series data on a **time-series graph**. Time goes along the x-axis, in order. The other variable goes up the y-axis. The points are joined with straight lines.

## Trends

A **trend** is the overall direction of a time-series graph. Read the graph from left to right.

- An **upward trend**: the values go up over time.
- A **downward trend**: the values go down over time.
- **No clear trend**: the values go up and down without a clear direction.

## Graphs in the media

Graphs in newspapers, on TV and online are made to tell a story. Read them carefully, like a statistician.

1. Read the title and the axis labels. What is being measured, and in what units?
2. Check the scale. Does it start at 0? Are the steps equal?
3. Describe what you see: the trend, the highest and lowest values, and where most of the values are.

### Example

A news graph shows a bakery’s pie sales going up from 50 to 60 a day. Its y-axis starts at 40, so the bar for 60 looks twice as tall as the bar for 50. But sales only went up by 10. A scale starting at 0 would show the real difference.

> **Watch out:** If a scale doesn’t start at 0, small changes can look huge.
