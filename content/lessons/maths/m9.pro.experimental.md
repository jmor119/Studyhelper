Some probabilities can't be worked out from equally likely outcomes. What's the chance a drawing pin lands point up, or a rugby kicker lands a conversion? The only way to find out is to experiment, and the more trials you do, the better your estimate gets.

## Relative frequency

Each time you do the experiment is a **trial**. The **relative frequency** of an outcome is

$$\text{relative frequency} = \frac{\text{number of times the outcome happened}}{\text{total number of trials}}$$

Relative frequency is also called **experimental probability**. It is an **estimate** of the true probability.

### Example

Sione drops a drawing pin 50 times. It lands point up 18 times.

Relative frequency of point up $= \dfrac{18}{50} = 0.36 = 36\%$.

So the probability of point up is **about** 0.36. Another 50 drops would probably give a slightly different answer.

## Comparing experiment with theory

When you *can* work out the theoretical probability, you can predict the **expected** number of times an outcome should happen:

$$\text{expected number} = \text{probability} \times \text{number of trials}$$

Roll a fair die 60 times and you'd expect about $\frac{1}{6} \times 60 = 10$ sixes. In a real experiment you might get 7 or 13. That's normal: results vary by **chance**, and repeated sets of trials give different results.

Reasons the experimental result might differ from the theoretical probability:

- **chance variation**, which is always there, especially with few trials
- **not enough trials**: use at least 30, and more is better
- the object might be **biased** (a bent coin or an uneven spinner)
- mistakes in recording results.

> **Watch out:** One surprising result doesn't prove a coin is unfair. 7 heads in 10 tosses happens quite often with a fair coin. 700 heads in 1000 tosses would be a different story.

## The Law of Large Numbers

The **Law of Large Numbers** says that as the number of trials increases, the relative frequency tends to get closer to the true probability.

Here's what one set of coin tosses might look like:

| Tosses | Heads | Relative frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 7 | 0.7 |
| 50 | 29 | 0.58 |
| 100 | 54 | 0.54 |
| 500 | 256 | 0.512 |
| 1000 | 497 | 0.497 |

With only 10 tosses the relative frequency is a long way from 0.5. By 1000 tosses it is very close. The results never settle exactly on 0.5, but the swings get smaller.

So:

- an estimate from **more trials** is more **reliable**
- combining the results of several groups gives a better estimate than any one group: add all the successes and divide by all the trials.

## Experiments and simulations

Some situations can **only** be explored by experiment, because the outcomes aren't equally likely and there's no way to calculate the probability:

- a drawing pin, bottle cap or paper cup landing a certain way
- an irregular 3D object, such as an oddly shaped lolly, landing on a particular face
- a sports player scoring from a certain spot.

A **simulation** uses something random (dice, a spinner, random numbers) to model a real situation, so you can do many trials quickly.

### Example

A netball shooter scores about 70% of her shots. To simulate her shots, generate random whole numbers from 1 to 10 (for example with a spreadsheet function such as `=RANDBETWEEN(1,10)`):

- 1 to 7 means a goal (7 out of 10 = 70%)
- 8 to 10 means a miss.

Each random number is one simulated shot. A spreadsheet can run hundreds of trials in seconds, which shows the Law of Large Numbers in action.

## Using your estimates

Once you have a relative frequency, you can use it to predict:

$$\text{estimated number} = \text{relative frequency} \times \text{number of trials}$$

### Example

Grace's pin landed point up 18 times in 50 drops. In 400 drops she'd estimate about $\frac{18}{50} \times 400 = 144$ point ups.

You can also estimate what's inside a bag. A bag holds 20 counters. Drawing one at random and putting it back 100 times gives 35 reds. About $\frac{35}{100}$ of the counters are red, so there are about $0.35 \times 20 = 7$ red counters.

> **Tip:** Always say "about" or "estimate". Experimental results vary, so your prediction is not exact.

## Exam tips

- **Relative frequency** = number of times ÷ total trials. Check the total by adding all the frequencies.
- **Expected number** = probability × trials. It is what you would expect on average, so real results will be close to it but rarely exactly equal.
- **Explain differences** between experiment and theory: chance variation, too few trials, or a biased object.
- **Law of Large Numbers:** more trials means a more reliable estimate. Combine results by adding totals, not by averaging the relative frequencies.
- **Simulations:** say exactly what each random number stands for, and how many trials you'll run.
