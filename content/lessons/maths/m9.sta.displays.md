A good display lets you see the pattern in data at a glance. Different kinds of data need different displays, so part of the skill is choosing the right one, and the other part is reading it accurately.

## Displays for categorical data

**Frequency tables** list each category with a count (its frequency). **Bar graphs** show the same thing visually:

- one bar per category, with **gaps** between the bars
- bar heights read from a labelled vertical axis that **starts at 0**
- a title and axis labels.

A **two-way table** shows two categorical variables at once, with totals:

| | Walk or bike | Car or bus | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Year 9** | 18 | 32 | 50 |
| **Year 10** | 25 | 25 | 50 |
| **Total** | 43 | 57 | 100 |

From it you can read, for example, that 18 Year 9 students walk or bike, and that 43 students altogether walk or bike.

> **Tip:** Check the totals: each row should add to its row total, and the bottom-right number is everyone.

## Dot plots and histograms

Numerical data has a **distribution**: how the values are spread along a scale.

A **dot plot** puts one dot for each value above a number line. Equal values stack up. It shows every value, so you can count dots, see the mode (tallest stack), and find the median by counting in from either end. Dot plots work best for small data sets.

A **histogram** groups numerical data into **class intervals** of equal width, such as 10–15, 15–20, 20–25 minutes. The height of each bar is the frequency for that interval.

- The bars **touch**, because the scale is continuous.
- A value on a boundary belongs to the interval it starts: 15 goes in 15–20, not 10–15.
- You can't read individual values from a histogram, only how many fall in each interval.

> **Watch out:** A bar graph (gaps, categories) and a histogram (no gaps, a number scale) can look alike. Check what's on the horizontal axis.

## Box plots

A **box plot** draws the five-number summary on a scale:

- whiskers from the **minimum** to $Q_1$ and from $Q_3$ to the **maximum**
- a box from $Q_1$ to $Q_3$, with a line at the **median**.

The four sections (left whisker, left part of the box, right part of the box, right whisker) each contain about **25%** of the values. So about 50% of the values are inside the box, and the width of the box is the IQR.

To draw one: find the five-number summary, draw a scale that covers the minimum to the maximum, then mark the five values and join them up.

> **Watch out:** A longer section does **not** contain more values. Every section has about a quarter of the data. A long section means those values are more spread out.

Box plots are great for **comparing** groups: draw them one above the other on the same scale.

## Scatter plots and lines of best fit

A **scatter plot** shows the relationship between two numerical variables measured on the same people or things. Each point is one person.

- The **explanatory variable** (the one that might explain or influence the other) goes on the **x-axis**.
- The **response variable** goes on the **y-axis**. For hours of study and test score, study time is explanatory and test score is the response.

Describe the pattern: **positive** (points tend to go up from left to right), **negative** (they tend to go down) or **no clear relationship**.

A **line of best fit** is drawn by eye through the middle of the points. It follows the trend, with about the same number of points above and below it. It doesn't have to go through the origin, or through any particular point. Use it to **predict**: go up from the x-value to the line, then across to the y-axis.

> **Tip:** A prediction from a line of best fit is an estimate. Real values are scattered around the line.

## Time-series graphs and choosing a display

A **time-series graph** shows how one variable changes over time. Time goes on the x-axis with equal spacing, and the points are joined with lines. Look for:

- a **trend**: the overall direction over a long time (rising, falling or steady)
- a **repeating pattern**, such as higher sales every summer.

Choosing a display:

| Data | Good display |
|---|---|
| One categorical variable | Bar graph, frequency table |
| Two categorical variables | Two-way table |
| One numerical variable | Dot plot, histogram, box plot |
| Comparing a numerical variable for two groups | Two box plots or dot plots on the same scale |
| Two numerical variables | Scatter plot |
| One variable over time | Time-series graph |

It often helps to make **several displays** for one investigation, such as a dot plot to see every value and a box plot to compare groups.

Choose sensible scales: start bar graph axes at 0, space the numbers evenly, and pick a scale that uses most of the space.

## Exam tips

- **Read the scale first.** Work out what one gridline is worth before reading any value.
- **Label everything** you draw: a title, both axes with units, and a key if needed.
- **Box plots:** each of the four sections holds about 25% of the values, whatever its length.
- **Histograms:** a value on a boundary goes in the interval that starts there.
- **Scatter plots:** explanatory variable on x, response on y. Describe the direction in context ("as height increases, arm span tends to increase").
- **Predictions:** show your reading lines on the graph, and say "about".
